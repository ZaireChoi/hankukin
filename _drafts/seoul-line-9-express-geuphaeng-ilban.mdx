Your train has been standing at the platform for ninety seconds with the doors open. Nobody else looks worried. Nobody is getting off. There is an announcement, in Korean, and it ends. Then another train comes through the station on the same track you are sitting on, does not stop, and disappears. Your doors close and you move.

Nothing went wrong. You are on a 일반 *(il-ban)* train, and you were just overtaken by a 급행 *(geup-haeng)* one. The operator of Seoul's Line 9 publishes a page explaining exactly this. It is on the Korean site.

## Two words, and only one of them is on the train you want

Once you can [sound out the letters](/en/hangul/learn-to-read-korean-signs-alphabet/), these two are worth learning as shapes rather than as words, because you will read them at a glance from a moving LED board:

| On the board | Sounds like | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **급행** | geup-haeng | Express. Skips stations. |
| **일반** | il-ban | Stops everywhere. |

The trap is not the vocabulary. It is that both trains use the same platform, in the same direction, on the same track. There is no separate express platform to fail to find. Whichever train arrives is the one at your feet, and the only thing distinguishing them is that word — on the platform display, on the front of the train, and in the announcement.

Note the second word. Korean has an older, more colloquial term for a stopping train, but the word Seoul Metro Line 9 actually prints is 일반, which literally means *general* or *ordinary*. If you are scanning for a word meaning "slow," you will not find one.

## Sixteen stops out of thirty-eight

Line 9 runs from Gaehwa in the far west to VHS Medical Center in the south-east. The operator lists 38 stations on the line, and marks 16 of them as 급행정차역 — express stopping stations:

Gimpo Int'l Airport · Magongnaru · Gayang · Yeomchang · Dangsan · Yeouido · Noryangjin · Dongjak · Express Bus Terminal · Sinnonhyeon · Seonjeongneung · Bongeunsa · Sports Complex · Seokchon · Olympic Park · VHS Medical Center

That leaves **22 stations the express does not stop at.** This is the practical consequence and it runs both ways. Board the express for a station that isn't on that list and you will be carried past it — on Line 9 that can mean several stops, not one. Stand on the platform waiting for a train to a station that *is* on the list, take the local, and you will get there, slowly, having been overtaken on the way.

One name on that list is worth a second look. **Express Bus Terminal** is the official English name of 고속터미널 station — it is named after the intercity coach terminal above it. The word "Express" there has nothing to do with the express train. It is a coincidence of translation, and it is the kind of thing that makes a reader distrust their own reading.

Gaehwa, the western terminus, is not among the 16.

## Why your local train stops and waits

This is the part that has no equivalent anywhere else most visitors have travelled, and it is the reason the doors stay open.

Some metro systems give their express trains their own pair of tracks. Line 9 does not. The operator's own description is that it runs local and express trains mixed on a single line, at a ratio of 1:1 — the two kinds of train share the rails. An express therefore cannot overtake a local wherever it likes; it has to do it at a station built with the room to allow it. That station is a 대피역 *(dae-pi-yeok)*, literally an "evacuation station," though what gets cleared is the track, not the train.

So: the wait is scheduled. Your train is not broken, the driver has not gone missing, and getting off to find another train is the one genuinely bad move available to you — the train you would be waiting for is the one that just went past without stopping.

## You cannot plan around it, and the operator says so

It would be convenient to give you the list of stations where this happens. The operator gives one, but it is published in time bands — six for the up direction, six for the down — and the stations change between them. In the first up-direction band, beginning 05:30, the passing stations are Gayang, Dongjak and Songpanaru. In the first down-direction band they are Sapyeong and Gayang. Different band, different stations.

Read that as a visitor. The operator's own instruction for handling the uncertainty is to listen to an announcement you cannot understand. That is not a complaint about Seoul Metro — the sentence is aimed at Korean commuters and it is good advice for them. It is a description of where you are standing. The workable version for you is the shape on the board, not the voice: if the train says 일반, expect a stop that lasts longer than a stop.

There is also a small crack in the page itself, which we mention because it tells you how firm these lists are. The up-direction table is headed "based on departure time from Gaehwa," and the note printed directly beneath it says "based on up-direction departure time from Gimpo Int'l Airport." Those are two different stations, one stop apart, in the same box. Whichever is meant, the times are a baseline for the whole line and not a promise about the platform you are on — which the page also tells you, two lines further down.

Two smaller things you will meet on that page and on Korean timetables generally. Directions are given as 상행 *(sang-haeng)*, up, and 하행 *(ha-haeng)*, down — not inbound and outbound, and not reliably mapped onto north, south or the end of the line you have in mind. We are deliberately not telling you which terminus counts as "up" on Line 9, because working it out from a timetable heading is exactly the kind of guess that goes wrong. Do with the direction word what you would do with an unfamiliar road sign: ignore it and read the station name printed next to it.

And Korean rail timetables run past midnight on the same clock, so the last down-direction band on this page begins at **24:19**, meaning 00:19 the following morning. A time written 24:19 is not a typo, and it is not tomorrow's train.

## The same word does not mean the same thing on the airport line

If you came in from Incheon you have already met a third word. The Airport Railroad runs 직통 *(jik-tong)* — direct — alongside its 일반 trains, and 직통 is stronger than 급행: it does not skip *some* stations, it stops at none. On the operator's weekday timetable the express runs Seoul Station to Terminal 1 in 43 minutes, every time, and on to Terminal 2 eight minutes later. There are 26 of them in each direction on a weekday.

That is a different product with a different ticket, and we have [set its fares against the bus and the taxi elsewhere](/en/now/incheon-airport-to-seoul-train-bus-taxi/). The point here is only that 급행 and 직통 are not synonyms, and the difference is the one that matters: an express will eventually stop somewhere, and a 직통 will not stop until the end.

## What the English site leaves out

We checked. Line 9's Korean site has a menu item called 열차이용안내 — "train use guide" — and it is the page every quotation above comes from. The English site's equivalent menu, Metro 9 Guide, contains Station Information, Schedules, Fare Guide, Convention Facilities and Lost & Found Office. There is no train use guide. The mechanism that makes your train sit still is explained in one language.

The English pages that do exist have been sitting there long enough to misspell themselves: the live menu reads *Station Infomation*, *Cusmomer Service* and *Roling stock*. We are not scoring anyone's English. We are pointing out that this is the tier of the site a visitor is routed to, and that the missing page is not a small one — it is the one that answers the question a visitor is most likely to have. This is the same pattern we found [across Korean institutions' English pages](/en/now/english-pages-korea-out-of-date/): the English page is not the Korean page translated.

## What this article does not claim

No journey time. The operator does not publish one on this page, and we will not compile it out of per-station timetables and call it official — so we cannot tell you how many minutes the express saves. We could not open an English Line 9 station timetable without guessing at a URL, so whether the English timetable labels express and local is unknown to us; the Korean one does. Nothing here is about fares, including whether the express carries a surcharge — [that piece is dated separately](/en/now/seoul-transport-fares-subway-bus-taxi/).

The station counts, the 1:1 mixing, the passing-station mechanism and the 05:30 examples are the operator's, checked 25 August 2026. That passing stations move is the operator's caveat, not ours.
