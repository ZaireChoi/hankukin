Eleven at night, a convenience store, a triangle gimbap in your hand. There is a date on the film, and above the date a word. Your camera-translation app renders it "expiration date." It renders the word on the milk beside it as "expiration date" too.

They are not the same word, they do not mean the same thing, and since 2023 only one of them is supposed to be there.

## 소비기한 is the only one the law bothers to define

Korea changed the word on its food packaging on **1 January 2023**. The old word, 유통기한 *(yu-tong-gi-han)*, was a distribution word: literally "circulation deadline," the date after which a shop could no longer sell the item. Whether you could still eat it was a separate question nobody printed. The new word, 소비기한 *(so-bi-gi-han)*, is a consumption word, and the Act on Labeling and Advertising of Foods spells out exactly what it promises:

Read the conditional clause twice, because it is the whole sentence. The date is not a property of the food. It is a promise about the food *on the assumption that you did what the label told you*. More on that below — it is the part that actually catches travellers.

The old word did not simply retire; it was deleted. Open the current Labeling Standards for Foods — the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety notice that governs what appears on a Korean package — and its definitions section names three date words: 제조연월일, 소비기한, 품질유지기한. **유통기한 is not among them.** We read the section; the word does not appear in it at all. It survives in the transitional clauses at the back of the document, which is the legal equivalent of a forwarding address.

## The number moved, and here is how far

This is the part that surprises people who assumed a relabelling exercise was cosmetic. Because 유통기한 had to leave room for the shop to sell the item *and* for you to eat it afterwards, the printed date sat well short of the real safety limit. 소비기한 does not have to leave that room. Same product, same factory, later date.

The Ministry ran setting experiments across food types and published the averages:

<ChartShelfLifeShift caption="Ministry of Food and Drug Safety reference-value experiments, published January 2023. Tofu moved least, at 36 per cent. Fruit and vegetable juice moved most, at 75 per cent — from twenty days to thirty-five. Averaged over the ten types, the number on the pack sits roughly half again as far out as the number it replaced.

Which produces an odd consequence, at least for the items the Ministry tested. Averaged across its four tofu samples, the date a shop may now sell to falls six days later than the date the same tofu would have carried in 2022. Nothing about the tofu changed. The question printed on it changed.

## Four words, and the Latin ones hiding next to them

Korean packages do you an unadvertised favour. The same MFDS standard that defines each date term also lists the English names and abbreviations a manufacturer may print — and many do, in small type beside or beneath the Hangul. If you learn nothing else here, learn to hunt for these:

| On the pack | Say it | What it is | Latin shorthand you may find |
|---|---|---|---|
| **소비기한** | so-bi-gi-han | Safe to eat until this date, if stored as told | Use by date, Expiration date, **EXP**, **E** |
| **품질유지기한** | pum-jil-yu-ji-gi-han | Quality holds until this date. Not a safety limit | Best before, Date of Minimum Durability, **BBE**, **BE** |
| **제조연월일** | je-jo-yeon-wol-il | When it was made | Date of Manufacture, **MFG**, **M**, **PRO**, **PROD**, **PRD** |
| **유통기한** | yu-tong-gi-han | The old sell-by. Should now appear only on milk — see below | *(no longer a defined term in the standard)* |

품질유지기한 is worth a moment, because it is the one people misread in the wrong direction. It goes on long-life goods — sugar, tinned things, some sauces — and it is a statement about flavour and texture, not about safety. The standard defines it as the period a food keeps "its inherent quality" under proper storage. A jar past its 품질유지기한 has not become dangerous; it has become disappointing. Korean law treats those as different claims, and so should you.

Reading the digits after the word is a separate skill, and a short one: Korean dates run largest unit first — year, then month, then day — and you will often see 까지 *(kka-ji)*, "until," closing the line. If the numerals themselves are giving you trouble, [Korean runs two entirely separate counting systems](/en/decode/korean-numbers-two-counting-systems/) and it is worth knowing which one you are looking at.

## The one carton that still says the old word

Here is the inversion. Of everything in a Korean chiller cabinet, the item where the difference between "safe to eat" and "allowed to sell" matters most is milk. Milk is also the single product that did not change.

The parent Act allowed a delay of up to eight years for categories needing better cold-chain handling; the Ordinance spent the full eight on chilled milk. So a carton in front of you today may carry 유통기한, entirely lawfully, and that date means the old thing — the last day for the shop, with the eating window unstated behind it.

We are not going to tell you how many days you personally have after that date, because nobody has published a number that applies to your carton, your journey home and your fridge. What we will say is that the two words on two products in the same cabinet are answering two different questions, and the milk is the one still answering the shop's.

## The date is a conditional, and the condition is written beside it

Back to that clause. 소비기한 holds "if the storage method shown on the food is complied with" — which is why the same standard requires the storage words to be on the pack, and why they are worth three minutes of your attention:

- **냉장** *(naeng-jang)* — keep chilled
- **냉동** *(naeng-dong)* — keep frozen
- **실온** *(sil-on)* — room temperature
- **보관방법** *(bo-gwan-bang-beop)* — the heading these appear under

A chilled item carried around Seoul in August, in a city that [logged 46 tropical nights in the summer of 2025 — the most since records began in 1908](/en/now/korea-summer-heat-humidity/), has not been stored the way the label specified, and the printed date has stopped promising anything. This is not pedantry imported from a rulebook; it is the literal scope of the sentence Korean law wrote.

Eggs make the point visible. The labeling standard requires egg packaging to carry a 소비기한, the storage method and temperature, and a printed line telling the buyer to refrigerate after purchase — and it permits the date to be expressed relative to the **산란일** *(san-ran-il)*, the lay date, as "○○ days from the lay date." The carton is showing you its own arithmetic.

If the Hangul itself is still a wall, that wall comes down faster than anyone expects: [the alphabet is a morning's work, and the consonants are diagrams of your mouth](/en/hangul/learn-to-read-korean-signs-alphabet/). And once you can sound out a package, the same trick works on the buildings — [Korean restaurant signs put the menu in the last word](/en/hangul/korean-restaurant-signs-last-word/).

## What this article does not claim

Every legal definition and date above is quoted or directly translated from the current text on the government's law portal, read on 20 August 2026: the Act on Labeling and Advertising of Foods (Article 2), its Enforcement Rule addenda (PM Ordinance 1813), and the MFDS Labeling Standards for Foods (Notice 2026-37). The transition date of 1 January 2023 is from the MFDS notice announcing it.

The chart is **averages of the Ministry's own setting experiments**, published January 2023, across between one and six items per food type. It is not a table of what any particular product says, and you should not use it to decide that a specific carton has more days in it than the carton claims.

Three things we deliberately left out. We have **not** printed the numeric temperature ranges that 냉장, 냉동 and 실온 correspond to: those live in the Food Code, which the law portal serves as a downloadable attachment we did not open, and we would rather give you the words than a number we half-checked. We have **not** verified which specific sub-types fall inside the 우유류 category that the milk exemption covers — the Ordinance defers that to a separate livestock-products standard we did not read. And we have **not** surveyed shelves to establish how many Korean milk cartons in August 2026 actually still print 유통기한; we can tell you what the law permits, not what any dairy chose to do.

Nothing here is food-safety advice for a particular item in your hand. It is a reading lesson for a word, and the word is 소비기한.
