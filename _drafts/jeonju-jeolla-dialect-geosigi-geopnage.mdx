You are at a counter in Jeonju and the person on the other side of it says <strong>우리 거그 안 갔다고?</strong> *(u-ri geo-geu an gat-da-go?)*

There is an <strong>안</strong> *(an)* in there. 안 is the negative — it is one of the first four or five words anyone learns. So the sentence is about not having gone somewhere.

It is not. The Academy of Korean Studies glosses that exact sentence as 우리 거기 갔잖아? — <em>we went there, didn't we?</em> The negative is sitting in the middle of a question that confirms you did.

That is the shape of the whole problem with the speech of the Jeolla provinces, and it is not the problem people expect. The trouble is not a wall of unfamiliar words. It is that a few very familiar ones are doing a different job here, and that two of the words most often held up as the sound of the region are classified by the national dictionaries in completely opposite ways.

This is the third of our regional pieces, after [Jeju](/en/decode/jeju-dialect-pokssak-sogatsuda-meaning/) and [Gyeongsang](/en/decode/busan-gyeongsang-dialect-endings-no-na/). Everything below comes from one signed encyclopaedia entry and the National Institute of Korean Language's two dictionaries, all opened on 9 September 2026.

## Two words, and the dictionaries only call one of them dialect

Take <strong>거시기</strong> *(geo-si-gi)* and <strong>겁나게</strong> *(geop-na-ge)*. Both belong to any list of Jeolla words you will ever be handed, so you would expect the national dictionaries to file them under the same heading. They file them under opposite ones.

<strong>거시기 is in the Standard Korean Dictionary, unmarked.</strong> It has two parts of speech there — a pronoun for a person or a thing whose name will not come to you, or that you would rather not say out loud, and an interjection for the same predicament mid-sentence. It has a sub-headword, <strong>거시기하다</strong>, and the Open Dictionary of Korean carries that as both a verb and an adjective. So Korean has a stand-in not only for the noun you have lost but for the verb and the adjective as well. English has *thingy* and *whatsit*; it does not have a way to say *I sort of whatsitted it*.

The classification is unambiguous. Search the Open Dictionary for 거시기 and it returns seven vocabulary items, and its own filter sorts them for you: <strong>seven general-vocabulary items, zero regional ones.</strong> There is no dialect label anywhere on the word. The dictionary also settles the spelling — 거시기 is standard and 거시키 is not, under article 4 of the Standard Language Regulations, which prefers the un-aspirated form.

<strong>겁나게 goes the other way, completely.</strong> The Standard Korean Dictionary returns exactly one headword for 겁나다: a verb, 怯나다, "to become frightened or afraid". That is all it has. But search the Open Dictionary for 겁나게 and you get two entries, both expert-reviewed, and both filed as <strong>regional (dialect)</strong> with zero general items:

| Headword | Part of speech | Gloss | Regions listed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 겁나게 「001」 | adverb | dialect for 매우 — *very* | South Jeolla, South Chungcheong |
| 겁나게 「002」 | adverb | dialect for 아주 — *very, quite* | Jeolla, South Chungcheong |

The encyclopaedia entry puts it more bluntly still: 겁나게 carries the sense of 굉장히, *enormously*, "rather than 무섭게", fearfully. So <strong>겁나게 맛있어</strong> is not *frighteningly tasty* with some dark edge to it. It is *really good*, and the fear is not in the sentence at all.

Two things worth taking from that table. South Chungcheong is in both rows, so this is not a Jeolla-only word and we are not going to call it one. And any dictionary that carries only 겁나다 will hand you the fear — which, as of today, includes the national standard one.

## 놈 is not the word your app thinks it is

This one is worth knowing before someone points at a fish for you.

Look up <strong>놈</strong> *(nom)* and the Standard Korean Dictionary gives you six senses as a bound noun. The first is "a derogatory word for a man", with 년 listed as its opposite. That is the sense that reaches subtitles, phrasebooks and translation apps, and it is why the word arrives in an English-speaking head somewhere around *bastard*.

The third sense in the same entry is quieter and nobody quotes it: "an offhand way of referring to a thing or an animal". The dictionary's own example is 큰 놈으로 골라 먹어라 — *pick a big one and eat it*. About sweet potatoes.

In the south-west, the encyclopaedia says, 놈 simply works as a bound noun with the same use as <strong>것</strong> *(geot)*, "thing" or "one". Its example is not even an object:

> 거그서 내려야 할 놈을 여까징 와 불었네.
> *The one I should have got off at, and I have come all the way here.*

The 놈 in that sentence is a bus stop. There is nobody in it to insult.

The useful distinction is register, and it is the one place our two sources do not quite line up. The standard dictionary marks the thing-sense as <em>offhand</em>. The dialect entry gives it as a plain equivalent of 것, with no such marking. We are not able to settle which is right for any particular speaker, and it does not matter much for you, because the instruction is the same either way: <strong>when 놈 is pointed at an object, no insult has been offered.</strong>

## Where the negative lands

Now back to the sentence at the counter, because this is the part that has no equivalent in anything you have been taught.

Two things are going on. First, in ordinary south-western negation the ending <strong>-지</strong> does not appear before 안. Standard Korean says 잡지 않아; the south-west says <strong>잡도 안해</strong>, with -도 (or -들, or -든) where -지 would be. So the negative sentence is already built differently.

Second, and stranger: the entry says 안 is also used in <strong>confirmation questions</strong>, and that it can appear after any phrase in the sentence.

The entry then gives the same question four ways, with 안 in four different places: 우리 거그 안 갔다고? · 안 우리 거그 갔다고? · 우리 안 거그 갔다고? · 우리 거그 갔다고 안? All four mean *we went there, didn't we?*

It also describes how it is spoken, which is the part a written explanation usually loses. In 우리 거그 안 갔다고?, the 안 is pronounced <strong>joined to the 거그 in front of it</strong> and broken away from the 갔다고 behind it. So the rhythm itself tells you it is not attached to the verb — which is exactly what your ear will assume if nobody says otherwise.

You are not going to produce this. But if you catch a floating 안 in a question and read it as a denial, you will agree to the opposite of what you meant. That is worth one paragraph of your attention.

## The table the encyclopaedia says it is about to print, and does not

Here is what happened when we went looking for the politeness endings, which is the thing that made [the Gyeongsang piece](/en/decode/busan-gyeongsang-dialect-endings-no-na/) worth writing.

The entry states that south-western addressee honorifics divide three ways — <strong>존대 · 평대 · 하대</strong> *(jon-dae · pyeong-dae · ha-dae)*, roughly deferential, level and plain. Then it says: "각각의 등급에 해당하는 대표적 종결어미를 간단히 표로 나타내 보이면 다음과 같다" — <em>the representative sentence endings for each grade, shown briefly in a table, are as follows.</em>

There is no table. We read the page's markup as well as its text; where the table should be there is an empty paragraph and then the next sentence. So the single most practical thing in the section — which ending belongs to which level — is not on the page, and <strong>we are not going to supply it from memory.</strong> If you want a worked example of what that table looks like when it does print, the Gyeongsang entry has one and we reproduced it.

What did survive is one sentence, and it happens to be the one that matters most to a visitor: <strong>the 평대 endings can be used to someone slightly senior to the speaker.</strong> The entry flags this as noteworthy, which tells you it is not how the standard system works. So a level of address that would be a small risk in Seoul is not necessarily one here, and if someone speaks to you at a pitch you cannot place, you should not read a slight into it.

Two other things in the grammar point the same direction. There is a subject honorific you will not recognise as one — <strong>-아게/어게-</strong>, which does the job of -(으)시- but only attaches before endings beginning with 아 or 어. 언능 와게 is 얼른 오셔, *do come quickly*, honorific and all; the entry traces the form back to the 계- of 계시다. And there is a particle <strong>-에가</strong> standing where standard Korean puts -에 or -에서: 어디에가 있냐?, *where is it?* Neither of those will look polite or even familiar, and one of them is.

If the levels themselves are new to you, [we drew the standard ladder here](/en/decode/banmal-jondaemal-when-casual-korean-is-rude/).

## The syllable that tells you which half of the region you are in

Jeolla is not one dialect. The entry divides it at the <strong>Noryeong range</strong> into a North Jeolla and a South Jeolla variety, and gives one marker for the split, in the summary of the article itself: standard Korean's <strong>-으니까</strong> (because) is broadly <strong>-응깨</strong> *(eung-kkae)* in the south and <strong>-응개</strong> *(eung-gae)* in the north.

Broadly is the entry's own hedge and we are keeping it. But it is a satisfying thing to be able to hear, because -으니까 is in a very large share of ordinary sentences, and a tensed 깨 against a plain 개 is an audible difference at a market stall. It is also a good reminder of the mistake we made a point of not making in Gyeongsang: <strong>we are not assigning any of this to a city.</strong> The entry works in provinces and sub-regions, and Jeonju, Gwangju, Yeosu and Mokpo are not named as the homes of any particular form.

One more from the same summary: the entry notes that many of the islands off the west and south coasts have distinct fishing-village features of their own, which is a caution against treating any of this as uniform.

## Sound changes that will hide a word you already know

None of these is slang. They are regular history, and each one can make a word you can read perfectly well arrive unrecognisable.

<strong>의 becomes 으.</strong> The entry says this applies broadly to 의 from Sino-Korean readings and to the possessive particle. Its examples: 의사 (a doctor) is <strong>으사</strong>, 우리의 is <strong>우리으</strong>. So the word for the thing you may most urgently need — a doctor, whose sign we cover in [reading clinic and pharmacy signs](/en/hangul/korean-clinic-pharmacy-signs/) — is one vowel off from the one you learned.

<strong>ㅎ does not aspirate what it meets.</strong> Where standard Korean turns ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ plus ㅎ into ㅋ, ㅌ, ㅍ, the south-west drops the ㅎ instead. 육학년 becomes <strong>유강년</strong>, 못하다 becomes <strong>모다다</strong>, 밥 한 그릇 becomes <strong>바반그럭</strong>.

<strong>Word-initial consonants tense.</strong> 걱정 becomes <strong>꺽정</strong> and 저기 becomes <strong>쩌그</strong>. That second one is worth memorising on its own, because it is the answer you will get to "where is it?" — someone will say 쩌그 and point.

<strong>Vowels correspond differently.</strong> 병아리 arrives as <strong>삥아리</strong>, 교통 as <strong>괴통</strong>, 모기 as <strong>모구</strong>. The entry also notes that ㄹ drops out of consonant clusters at the end of a verb stem, so 읽다 is said 익따 and 짧다 is 짭따.

For the record, the entry gives the region ten simple vowels — 이, 에, 애, 위, 외, 으, 어, 아, 우, 오 — with one exception it names: in western South Jeolla and in Gwangju, ㅔ and ㅐ are not kept apart. It also says awareness of vowel length is comparatively strong here. We are not going to turn either of those into advice, because neither is something you can act on at a counter; they are here so that the ten-vowel claim is not floating on its own.

## The ending that is only warmth

We will finish on the one piece of the dialect that has no trap in it.

The entry singles out <strong>-잉</strong> *(-ing)* as the region's most characteristic manner of speech: the vowel 이 with a nasal laid over it, stuck on the end of a sentence or of any phrase inside it. Its stated function is not grammatical. It shows, in the entry's words, the affection the speaker feels toward the person they are talking to. The example it gives has three of them in one breath: 나도잉, 헌닥 허먼잉, 꼭 허는 사람잉께잉.

That is the note to end on, because it is the corrective to the thing this article has otherwise been about. Almost everything above is a warning that a Jeolla sentence may not mean what its parts suggest. The most distinctive feature in it means exactly what it sounds like.

## You are not expected to speak any of it

The advice is the same as it was for Jeju and for Gyeongsang. The dialect changes from province to province; what you should do about it does not.

Keep putting 요 on the end of your own sentences, exactly as in [our phrases for shops and restaurants](/en/decode/korean-phrases-shops-restaurants/). Standard Korean is understood throughout Jeolla, nobody expects a visitor to produce -응깨 or -잉, and reaching for a dialect form you have read about once is a reliable way to say something you did not mean. You are being asked to recognise this, not to perform it.

And there is a reason to go. Of the eight everyday dishes the Korean government prices in every province, [naengmyeon is nearly ₩3,000 cheaper in South Jeolla than in Seoul](/en/now/korea-food-prices-what-meals-cost/) — while bibimbap costs more in North Jeolla (Jeonbuk) than in the capital, because Jeonju is where it is from and charges accordingly. If you cannot read the counter yet, [start with the alphabet](/en/hangul/learn-to-read-korean-signs-alphabet/).

## What this article does not claim

The dialect material is from a single signed entry in the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture — 「서남방언」, written by Jeong Seung-cheol and last revised on 28 August 2026. The Academy of Korean Studies states that such entries carry their author's scholarly views rather than an institutional position. The dictionary material is from the National Institute of Korean Language's Standard Korean Dictionary and Open Dictionary of Korean, read the same day.

We have <strong>not</strong> attached any form to any city. Where the entry says North Jeolla or South Jeolla, we have said that.

We make <strong>no claim about how any of this sounds</strong> to a foreign listener, and none about how many people use any of these forms today; the entry describes a dialect and is not a survey.

Two things we looked for and did not find. The entry's table of politeness endings <strong>is not on the page</strong> — the text announces it and nothing follows — so no such table appears above. And 시방 *(si-bang)*, which we checked on the way, turns out to sit in the Standard Korean Dictionary as 時方, a plain word for the present moment, with no dialect label on any of the four headwords it returns. We could not source it as a regional form, so it does not appear above as one.
