You are at a convenience store counter. The cashier holds up your two bottles of water and says something ending in a rising tone. You catch the word 봉투 *(bong-tu)*, bag, and you would like one, so you say 네 *(ne)*.

You walk out holding two loose bottles.

What she asked was 봉투 안 필요하세요? — *do you not need a bag?* And 네 did not mean *yes, I want one*. It meant *correct, I do not*.

This is not a trick of politeness or a cultural subtlety. It is the dictionary definition of the word, and Korea's national dictionary states it in one line.

What that line does not do — and this is the part nobody tells you — is settle every case. There are two kinds of Korean question with a negative in them, they take opposite answers, and when a Korean speaker asked the National Institute of Korean Language to explain the difference, the institute said that was not something it could answer.

## The definition points at the question, not at the fact

Look up 네 in the 표준국어대사전 *(pyo-jun-gug-eo-dae-sa-jeon)*, the Standard Korean Dictionary published by the National Institute of Korean Language, and the interjection entry reads:

  ko="윗사람의 부름에 대답하거나 묻는 말에 긍정하여 대답할 때 쓰는 말."
  en="A word used to answer when an elder calls you, or to answer affirming the words that were asked."

  whoEn="National Institute of Korean Language, Standard Korean Dictionary, 네 as an interjection"

  url="https://stdict.korean.go.kr/search/searchResult.do?searchKeyword=%EB%84%A4" />

묻는 말에 긍정하여 — *affirming the words that were asked.* Not affirming the situation. Not affirming what you want. Affirming **the sentence the other person just said**.

아니요 *(a-ni-yo)* is the exact mirror image, and its entry says so with the same construction: 윗사람이 묻는 말에 부정하여 대답할 때 쓰는 말 — a word used when answering by negating the words an elder asks.

Neither definition mentions reality at all. Both of them are about the question. English works the other way: *yes* and *no* attach to the underlying fact, which is why an English speaker answering *no* to "you don't want a bag?" is confirming the same thing a Korean speaker confirms with 네.

So the rule most guides print — *네 means "that's right", not "yes"* — is true. It is just not the whole story, and the rest of the story is where this stops being trivia.

## Two questions that look the same and take opposite answers

Korean has two different things that both come out of a speaker's mouth sounding like a negative question, and the National Institute of Korean Language answers them differently.

A reader wrote in to the institute's public language-advice board asking what to say to 무섭지 않아? *(mu-seop-ji an-a)* — *aren't you scared?* — if the answer is that you are not. The reply, published 6 January 2025, opened by refusing to give a single answer:

<Original
  ko="제시문의 경우 표현 의도에 따라 답이 달라질 수 있습니다."
  en="In the case of the sentence given, the answer can change depending on the intended meaning."
  who="국립국어원 온라인가나다 답변"
  whoEn="National Institute of Korean Language, Online Ganada advisory reply"
  date="2025-01-06"
  url="https://www.korean.go.kr/front/onlineQna/onlineQnaView.do?mn_id=216&qna_seq=308639" />

The reply then splits that one sentence into its two possible readings, and attaches a definition from the institute's own dictionaries to each.

| | What it is | Marked by, with the institute's own example | Then 무섭지 않아? answered 응 *(eung)* means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **확인 의문문** *(hwag-in ui-mun-mun)* | A confirmation question. You already believe something and are checking it | **-지 않-** · 김 선생이 그 분야의 권위자이지 않니? | **Yes, I am scared** — it attaches to the positive |
| **부정 의문문** *(bu-jeong ui-mun-mun)* | A genuine question that happens to be in negative form | **안** *(an)* or **못** *(mot)* · 철수는 안 왔니? · 철수를 아직도 못 만났니? | **Right, I am not scared** — it attaches to the negative |

응 is the casual form; 네 is its polite equivalent and behaves the same way. So read the right-hand column twice. For the identical English sentence *aren't you scared?*, 네 means **"yes I am"** in one reading and **"no I'm not"** in the other, and nothing in the tone of voice tells you which one you just heard.

The good news is that the two kinds usually look different on the page. The dictionary entry for 확인 의문문 says outright that it takes -지 않-; the entry for 부정 의문문 gives examples built with 안 and 못. That is a listening cue you can actually use: **hear 안 or 못 in the question and 네 will mean "correct, I don't."**

The bad news is the next section.

## Korea's own language institute would not reduce it to a rule

In March 2025 a Korean speaker wrote to the same board with a sharper version of the question. They gave two sentences meaning roughly the same thing — one built with -지 않-, one built with 안 — said that as a native speaker they answered the first with 어 *(eo)* and the second with 아니, without thinking about it, and asked what the underlying rule was.

The institute declined. It said the question fell outside what the board answers, being a matter of cultural response rather than of spelling or standard-language regulation, and pointed the writer at academic literature. Then it added one sentence of its own:

That is the honest position, from the body that decides what standard Korean is: the form does not settle it, and context can flip it.

We are pointing this out because every English-language explanation of Korean *yes* and *no* we have seen presents a clean rule with no exceptions. The clean rule is a simplification of something the national language institute would not simplify for a Korean who asked.

## So stop answering yes or no

The practical conclusion is not *learn to distinguish the two question types under pressure at a counter*. It is that **네 and 아니요 are the two worst possible answers to a question with a negative in it**, and Korean gives you a better one.

Answer with the verb. Korean sentences end in the verb, and repeating it — positive or negative — cannot be misread, because it states the fact instead of scoring the question.

| You hear | Do not say | Say |
|---|---|---|
| 봉투 안 필요하세요? *(bong-tu an pi-ryo-ha-se-yo)* — don't you need a bag? | 네 | **필요해요** *(pi-ryo-hae-yo)* I need one · **필요 없어요** *(pi-ryo eop-seo-yo)* I don't |
| 예약 안 하셨어요? *(ye-yak an ha-syeo-sseo-yo)* — you didn't book? | 아니요 | **했어요** *(hae-sseo-yo)* I did · **안 했어요** *(an hae-sseo-yo)* I didn't |
| 카드 안 되세요? *(ka-deu an doe-se-yo)* — is your card not working? | 네 | **안 돼요** *(an dwae-yo)* it isn't · **다시 해 볼게요** *(da-si hae bol-ge-yo)* I'll try again |
| 아직 안 드셨어요? *(a-jik an deu-syeo-sseo-yo)* — you haven't eaten yet? | 네 | **먹었어요** *(meo-geo-sseo-yo)* I have · **아직 안 먹었어요** *(a-jik an meo-geo-sseo-yo)* not yet |

Two of these are worth saying out loud before you fly. They are the rows where the wrong answer costs more than a bag: a booking you did make being written down as one you did not, and a card that would have gone through on a second attempt being written off on the first.

There is a second reason this works. Repeating the verb keeps you inside the polite 해요체 *(hae-yo-che)* forms without having to think about level, which matters more in Korea than the choice of word does — see [when casual Korean turns into an insult](/en/decode/banmal-jondaemal-when-casual-korean-is-rude/). 네 and 아니요 are already polite; 응 and 아니 *(a-ni)* are the casual pair, and they are not interchangeable with a stranger.

## One spelling note, because two spellings are in circulation

In writing, the pair is often 예 / 아니요 rather than 네 / 아니요. 예 *(ye)* is a synonym of 네 — the dictionary entry for 네 gives 예 as its equivalent — and the institute's own worked example of a written instruction is *answer with 예/아니요*.

Which brings up the one spelling that catches Koreans too. 아니요 and 아니오 are both real words and they are not the same word, and the institute's newsletter said in 2003 that many people were confused between them. The answer *no* is 아니요: the interjection 아니 plus the polite particle 요. 아니오 is the adjective 아니다 with the ending -오, as in *he is not an employee* — a sentence, not an answer. If you meet 아니오 on a printed button, that is the mix-up, and the button still means no. In speech 아니요 usually contracts to 아뇨 *(a-nyo)*, which is what you will actually hear.

The rest of the sentences that surround these answers at a till are in [what to say in shops and restaurants](/en/decode/korean-phrases-shops-restaurants/), and the counter where a mistaken yes is most expensive has its own vocabulary in [refunds and cancellations](/en/decode/korea-refund-hwanbul-cheongyakcheolhoe/).

## What this article does not claim

**The 안/못 versus -지 않- cue is a reading of the dictionary entries, not a rule the institute states as a rule.** The entries describe each question type with those forms; the institute's own advisory reply says context can override the form. Treat the cue as a way to notice you are in danger, not as a decoder.

**We have not verified what any particular shop, hotel or station actually says.** The Korean sentences in the tables above are ours, written to the patterns in the dictionary examples. They are the shapes to listen for, not transcripts.

**This is standard Korean only.** Regional speech and set phrases behave differently, and we have not checked them here.
