The toilet is not inside the café. The barista points down a corridor, past the lift, to a grey door with a keypad on it. You press the handle. Nothing. You press it again. Behind you someone from the office upstairs walks up, taps four digits without looking, and goes in. The four digits were printed at the bottom of the receipt you dropped in the bin on the way out.

That door is the first of three places in Korea where the toilet has a rule attached and nobody thinks to tell you, because every Korean already knows. The second is the stall, where a national decree decides where the paper goes and a panel of buttons is labelled only in Hangul. The third is the subway gate, where going out for the toilet used to cost a second fare and, since 2023 in Seoul and since June 2026 on the rest of the network, mostly does not — with one exception that catches tourists specifically.

## The word on the door means "make-up room"

화장실 *(hwa-jang-sil)* is the word to recognise first, and it does not say toilet. Its three syllables are 化粧室, a make-up room, and the Standard Korean Language Dictionary lists that sense first — a room fitted out for doing one's make-up — before the second sense, "another way of saying 변소," the plain old word for a privy. The euphemism won so completely that the plain word has almost vanished from signs. If you have sounded out [the alphabet](/en/hangul/learn-to-read-korean-signs-alphabet/), 화장실 is the string to scan a corridor for; the arrow next to it does the rest.

Then the two doors. The Public Toilets Act is blunt about them:

What the law does not fix is the lettering, so the doors use several pairs and you should know all of them. The shortest is one syllable each: 남 *(nam)* and 여 *(yeo)*, men and women. Longer doors spell out 남자 *(nam-ja)* and 여자 *(yeo-ja)*. Formal buildings and older hotels use 신사 *(sin-sa)* and 숙녀 *(sung-nyeo)*, gentlemen and ladies. And a fair number of doors skip Hangul entirely for the Chinese characters 男 and 女, which is the one pair a reader from Japan or China will already know and a reader from anywhere else will not. Most doors also carry the usual blue-and-red pictograms, but not all — the ones that rely on text alone are exactly where a wrong guess is most embarrassing. A third door, 장애인 화장실 *(jang-ae-in hwa-jang-sil)*, is the accessible toilet, which the same Article 7 requires; it is normally unlocked and single-occupancy.

## The code is on the receipt

Here is the part no statute covers, so we will say plainly that it is observation. In a great many cafés and office buildings in Korean cities, the toilet is not inside the shop but in the shared corridor, and the door is locked with a keypad so the building's tenants are not paying to clean up after the whole street. The code is given to customers in one of two ways: printed at the bottom of the receipt, or on a small card at the counter. A sign on the door will say something close to 화장실 비밀번호는 영수증 하단에 있습니다 — the toilet 비밀번호 *(bi-mil-beon-ho)*, literally the secret number, is at the bottom of the 영수증 *(yeong-su-jeung)*, the receipt. Both nouns are worth learning because they are what you will need to say back: 화장실 비밀번호가 뭐예요? *(hwa-jang-sil bi-mil-beon-ho-ga mwo-ye-yo)* — what is the toilet code? If you did not buy anything, that question is also, politely, the moment to buy something. The [phrases for shops and counters](/en/decode/korean-phrases-shops-restaurants/) cover the rest of the exchange.

There is a second kind of door that opens without a code, and the law created it. Article 9 lets a district designate toilets in private buildings above a certain size as 개방화장실 *(gae-bang hwa-jang-sil)*, open toilets, and then obliges the district to mark them:

So a sticker reading 개방화장실 on an office lobby, a petrol station or a shop front means you may walk in and use the toilet without being a customer, and the building agreed to that with the district. The design of the sticker is left to each city, which is why we cannot show you one; the four syllables are what to read.

## Where the paper goes

Older guidebooks, and a few older signs, tell you to put used paper in a bin beside the toilet rather than flush it. That instruction is now the opposite of the national management standard for public toilets. The decree under the Act says, in its list of rules for whoever manages the toilet:

<Original
  ko="대변기 칸막이 안에는 휴지통을 두지 아니할 것."
  en="No waste bin shall be placed inside a toilet cubicle."
  who="공중화장실 등에 관한 법률 시행령 제7조 제3호"
  whoEn="Enforcement Decree of the Public Toilets Act, Article 7, item 3"
  date="2026-09-06"
  url="https://www.law.go.kr/법령/공중화장실등에관한법률시행령" />

The same article then says what stays: a box for sanitary products in the women's cubicles, 위생용품 수거함 *(wi-saeng-yong-pum su-geo-ham)*, and a bin only where the cubicle is an accessible one or has a nappy-changing table. So the modern sign above the roll reads 휴지는 변기에 *(hyu-ji-neun byeon-gi-e)* — paper into the bowl — and the small box in the women's stall is not for paper.

Two cautions, and they are the kind this site exists to give. First, the decree governs 공중화장실등, the public toilets the Act applies to — stations, terminals, parks, large shops, designated open toilets and so on. A private café's toilet is outside it, and some still have a bin and a sign asking you to use it. Second, the verb decides everything. 버려 주세요 *(beo-ryeo ju-se-yo)* is "please put it in"; 버리지 마세요 *(beo-ri-ji ma-se-yo)* is "please do not." The 마세요 ending negates whatever comes before it, so a sign with 변기 and 마세요 in it means the opposite of a sign with 변기 and 주세요. Read to the end of the line before you act on the first noun.

## Four words on the bidet, and the one to find first

The seat has a control panel on the side or on the wall, and it is labelled in Korean. This part is observation rather than law, and panel layouts differ between makers, but four words cover almost every panel we have seen. 세정 *(se-jeong)*, wash, starts the rear jet. 비데 *(bi-de)*, the loanword bidet, is the front wash. 건조 *(geon-jo)* is the warm-air dryer. And 정지 *(jeong-ji)* is stop. Locate 정지 before you press anything else; it is the only button whose position you need to remember, and it is often the one printed in a different colour. Do not count on standing up to stop the jet — find the word. Pressure and nozzle-position buttons are usually marked 수압 *(su-ap)* and 노즐 *(no-jeul)* with plus and minus signs, which you can read already.

## Two more things on the wall

A red or orange button at hand height inside the stall, sometimes labelled 비상벨 *(bi-sang-bel)*, is an emergency bell, not a flush. The Act added it in 2021, in force from July 2023: districts must fit public toilets with a bell that connects to the facility's manager or the local police station for a fast response. Press it only if you mean it. The flush is on the tank, or on some seat panels as a button marked 물내림 *(mul-nae-rim)*, literally "water down."

And a board at the entrance reading 청소 중 *(cheong-so jung)*, cleaning in progress, explains a situation that startles visitors: a woman cleaning the men's toilet, or occasionally the reverse. The decree requires exactly that board — when a manager of one sex enters the other's toilet to clean or repair, a sign must be placed at the entrance so users know. The cleaner is not lost, and neither are you. Go in, or wait a minute; both are normal.

## Fifteen minutes, and the ticket that does not count

The place a toilet trip actually costs money is the subway, and here two operators run two nearly identical rules that took three years to line up. On the Seoul-run lines, going out through the gates to find a toilet and coming back in used to mean paying the base fare twice. From 1 July 2023 Seoul let riders tap out and tap back in at the same station within ten minutes without a new base fare, and from 7 October 2023 it stretched that to fifteen minutes. That left a gap. Lines 1, 3 and 4 change operator as they leave the city, and on the Korail-run stretches — plus the Suin-Bundang, Gyeongui-Jungang, Gyeonggang and Seohae lines — there was no such rule until this summer: riders either called a staff member to the emergency gate or, as the ministry admits many did rather than ask, paid the base fare twice. The conditions are the same on both sides: same station, same line, once per journey, and it is processed as a transfer — so if you continue further than the base distance you still pay the distance supplement described in the [fares article](/en/now/seoul-transport-fares-subway-bus-taxi/). The exclusions are where the tourist is caught. Both Seoul and the ministry apply the rule to transport cards only; the single-journey ticket, 1회권 *(il-hoe-gwon)*, the one bought from the machine for a single ride, is not covered, and neither is a period pass. A single-journey rider who needs the toilet must do what everyone did before 2023: press the intercom at the wide 비상게이트 *(bi-sang-ge-i-teu)*, the emergency gate beside the turnstiles, and ask the attendant. The ministry's release also lists the lines that are outside the scheme altogether — the airport railroad, the Shinbundang line, Gimpo Gold Line and the light rail lines — so on your first ride in from Incheon no such rule exists at all.

If the station's toilet is inside the paid area, none of this applies; look for 화장실 on the [platform signage](/en/hangul/seoul-subway-signs-name-on-the-glass/) before you tap out, because once you are through the gates the clock is running.

## What this article does not claim

The law and decree texts above were read on 6 September 2026 at the Korea Law Information Center, and the subway rules in the Seoul and ministry releases linked in the sources. Where the article says "in many cafés," "on most panels" or "some still have a bin," that is our observation from doors, stalls and seats, not a survey, and we make no claim about any particular building. Keypad codes, sticker designs and bidet layouts vary; the words do not. The 15-minute rule's exact station list on the Korail side is in the ministry release and may change — check it if the difference matters to you.
