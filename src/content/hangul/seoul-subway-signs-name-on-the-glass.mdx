In 2022, the complaint desk at Seoul's subway operator — a channel called 고객의 소리 *(go-gaek-ui so-ri)*, the Voice of the Customer — took in 819 complaints in a single year asking for one thing: make it easier to tell which station the train is at. The complaints were filed in Korean, by people who ride the system every day. One of them, quoted in the city's own press release, reads like the inside of every visitor's head:

Hold on to that. The feeling of a platform sliding past while an announcement dissolves into train noise is not your Korean failing you — locals filed 819 complaints a year about it, in their own language. What they got in response, from 2023, is the most visitor-friendly thing in the system, and no sign points it out. We will get to it. First, the handful of words that hang from the ceiling.

## Four signs hold a station up

Seoul's overhead signage is heavily bilingual — on the big boards the English is right there, and it is good. The Korean below is still worth thirty seconds of your time, for the pillar signs, the floor arrows, and the moments when no board happens to be facing you. Three of the four are not dictionary compounds but plain, homely Korean:

| On the sign | Sounds like | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 타는 곳 | ta-neun got | The platform. Literally "the riding place." |
| 나가는 곳 | na-ga-neun got | The way out — "the going-out place." |
| 갈아타는 곳 | ga-ra-ta-neun got | The transfer corridor — "the place to change your riding." |
| 환승 | hwan-seung | Transfer again, in one compact word — this is the form the announcements use. |

Two habits attach to these. Exits under 나가는 곳 are numbered, and the number is the whole address: a Seoul meeting point is "exit 4," not a street name, and two exits of the same station can surface a serious walk apart — so agree on the digit, not the station. And transfer routes are marked by line number and colour the entire way; you follow 갈아타는 곳 plus a colour, not a compass.

## 방면 points with a name you are not going to

The word that decides which side of the platform you stand on is 방면 *(bang-myeon)* — "toward." Here is the trap: the sign does not list your destination. It names a station further down the line, often the end of the line, and if you have never heard of that station the sign feels useless. It is not. You are not being asked to know where that station is — you are being asked to check the line diagram on the platform wall, find your own station on it, and see whether it lies in the direction of the name on the sign. The 방면 name is an arrow, not an itinerary.

On Line 2, the green circle line, you will also meet 내선 *(nae-seon)* and 외선 *(oe-seon)* — the inner and the outer loop. We are deliberately not teaching you to decode them: which physical track counts as inner is exactly the kind of thing that costs a wrong-direction ride to get wrong, and the words are very much alive — the city's own 2023 press release locates the first station-name installation by writing "2호선 외선 방향," Line 2, outer-loop direction. You do not need the decoding. The platform sign that says 방면, and the next-station name posted with it, answer the only question you actually have: does this side go toward my station or away from it? Board the wrong way on an ordinary line and you have bought the same ride twice; on a loop, you can ride the long way around a city.

## The name on the glass

Now the 2023 fix, because it changes what you should do with your eyes on board. The city and Seoul Metro began fixing the station's name to the 승강장안전문 *(seung-gang-jang-an-jeon-mun)* — the platform screen doors, the glass wall between platform and track — in two sizes, positioned for the two kinds of rider. The doors that slide open with the train (가동문, *ga-dong-mun*) carry a small name sign at standing eye level. The fixed emergency panels between them (비상문, *bi-sang-mun*) carry a large one, sized to be read from a seat, through the train window.

Why this matters more to you than to the commuters who asked for it: the glass sign is fixed to the station, not to the train. It cannot preview, advertise or cycle. When you wake from a doze, or the announcement drowned, or your ears simply are not fast enough in Korean yet — the name in your window is the station you are actually at, every time. Two syllables of Hangul at that size are readable within your first hour of [learning the alphabet](/en/hangul/learn-to-read-korean-signs-alphabet/), and station names are the single best flashcards in the country: short, everywhere, and self-checking. One caution while you stare at the glass — if you leave something behind the doors, staff [will not open them for you](/en/now/lost-something-in-korea-what-to-do/); that is a different article.

## 이번 역, 다음 역 — one word of difference

The screens inside the car are the reason the glass is the better habit. They cycle, and the station name on screen is usually prefixed by one of two phrases: 이번 역 *(i-beon yeok)*, this station, or 다음 역 *(da-eum yeok)*, the next one. Catch the name without its prefix and you can get off one stop early with complete confidence — the screen was telling you about a station the train had not reached. The press release itself lists the in-car displays among the things riders complained about. So read the prefix, or skip the contest: the screen describes two stations, the glass only ever describes one.

Two more notices live inside the car and rarely travel with English. 약냉방칸 *(yak-naeng-bang-kan)* marks a car where the air conditioning is turned down on purpose — if your car in August feels broken, it may be labelled, and the cure is walking one car along. And the pink seat marked 임산부 배려석 *(im-san-bu bae-ryeo-seok)* is set aside for pregnant passengers; the custom is to leave it empty even when the car is full, and the polite visitor does. One platform word we are sending you elsewhere for: 급행 *(geup-haeng)*, express, [skips stations — and on Line 9 it decides whether your train stops at all](/en/hangul/seoul-line-9-express-geuphaeng-ilban/).

## What this article does not claim

The 819 complaints are the 2022 figure; the rollout — City Hall first, all 337 Seoul stations by end of July 2023 — is the city's published schedule, and we found no later announcement confirming completion, so we do not promise every platform in every direction carries the new signs, nor whether each sign adds romanization; the Hangul is the constant. Where signs and screens typically hang, that overhead signage is bilingual, and how exits are used as meeting points are our observation from the system, not a survey. The word glosses are our translations of everyday compounds, not dictionary citations. Both city pages we quote were read on 28 August 2026 and are linked above; nothing here is about fares, which [are dated separately](/en/now/seoul-transport-fares-subway-bus-taxi/).
