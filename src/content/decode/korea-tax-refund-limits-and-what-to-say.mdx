There is a sticker on the door that says TAX FREE, and there is a sticker two doors down that says TAX REFUND, and in Korean these are not two ways of saying the same thing. One of them means the tax was never added to the price. The other means the tax is in the price you are about to pay, and you will get it back later if you do four things in the right order before you leave the country.

Which of those two you are standing in front of decides whether you need your passport out at the till. English flattens the difference; Korean does not.

## 환급 is not 환불

Start with the word, because it is the one that keeps the rest straight.

**환불** *(hwan-bul)* is a refund of a purchase. You bring the thing back, you get your money. That is [the seven-day question we looked at separately](/en/decode/korea-refund-hwanbul-cheongyakcheolhoe/), and it has nothing to do with tax.

**환급** *(hwan-geup)* is a return of tax you have already paid. It is the word in every institutional name attached to this system — 사후면세점 <strong>즉시환급</strong>제도 at the policy briefing, 내국세<strong>환급</strong> on the airport's own menu, 관세<strong>환급</strong> at the customs service. Not one of them says 환불. English says "refund" for both, which is why a traveller can read three guides and still not know whether they are owed goods, money, or tax.

What you are reclaiming is 부가가치세 *(bu-ga-ga-chi-se)*, value-added tax, and on some goods an individual consumption tax on top of it. It is inside the shelf price. You do not see it come off unless someone takes it off.

| The sign or the word | Sounds like | What it actually means |
|---|---|---|
| 면세점 | myeon-se-jeom | Duty free shop — tax never charged. Lotte, Shilla, Shinsegae; airports and downtown |
| 사후면세점 | sa-hu-myeon-se-jeom | "After-the-fact duty-free shop" — an ordinary shop. You pay tax, then reclaim it |
| 즉시환급 | jeuk-si-hwan-geup | Immediate refund — the tax comes off at the till |
| 환급 | hwan-geup | Getting tax back |
| 환불 | hwan-bul | Getting purchase money back. Not this |
| 택스리펀 | taek-seu-ri-peon | "Tax refund", borrowed whole. The word people say out loud |
| 여권 | yeo-gwon | Passport |

Both kinds of shop have the syllables 면세 in the name, and both put English on the glass. The one that matters to you day to day is the second kind — the 사후면세점, which is a chemist, a clothes shop, a supermarket, a convenience store. Korea has been letting these take the tax off at the till since 1 January 2016, and the Korea Tourism Organization says the scheme has spread from department stores and large supermarkets down to smaller shops and some convenience stores.

## One receipt, three doors

Here is the part no single page states in one place. There are three ways to get the tax back, and **the amount on one receipt decides which of them are open to you.** The Korea Tourism Organization publishes all three limits — but in three separate paragraphs, so the shape never appears.

The immediate refund at the till shuts at ₩1,000,000; the downtown booth shuts at ₩6,000,000; the airport counter has no ceiling. Steps are drawn equal width, so the picture shows order and not proportion." />

Read it as a ladder. A ₩40,000 cosmetics purchase can take any of the three doors, and the till is the easiest. A ₩1,500,000 coat cannot use the till at all — nobody has done anything wrong, that receipt simply belongs at a downtown booth or the airport. A ₩7,000,000 purchase has exactly one door left.

Three details the chart cannot carry:

- The immediate refund has a **second** cap that is not a receipt amount: ₩5,000,000 across your whole trip. Stay under ₩1,000,000 per payment and you can still run out.
- A downtown booth only handles **its own company's** receipts — Global Blue, GLOBAL TAX FREE, Easy Tax Refund, CubeRefund and the rest are separate operators. Airport and port counters take all of them.
- Taking a refund downtown does not end it. You still have to report the refund to customs when you fly out. It is an advance, not a settlement.

## Whether you are a tourist is a question about months

The eligibility test is not about your passport alone. Per the Korea Tourism Organization: a non-Korean citizen qualifies if they have been in Korea **less than six months**; an overseas Korean qualifies if they have been in Korea less than three months *and* have lived abroad more than two years or hold permanent residency elsewhere.

That six-month line is the one that catches people who did not think of themselves as affected. An exchange student in month seven is buying at the same till as everyone else and is no longer eligible, whatever the sticker on the door says.

The rest of the test is about the goods and the clock: the purchase must be **₩15,000 or more**, the goods must be **new, unopened and unused**, and you must **leave Korea within three months** of the purchase date. Unopened is not a formality — at the airport a customs officer may ask to see the thing.

## The number Korea's own English pages disagree on

This is where we have to show our working rather than give you a figure.

The Korea Tourism Organization's page — which carries a note saying it was last updated on 21 October 2025 — puts the minimum purchase at **₩15,000**, and repeats that figure in several places on the same page.

The Incheon International Airport Corporation's English tax-refund page tells you to ask for a refund form "after making your purchase (over 30,000 KRW)" — **double that**.

Both are Korean public bodies. Both pages were open in front of us on 2 September 2026. The likeliest reading is that the airport's page is simply old. Korea.net, the government's own news service, reported in November 2023 that the finance ministry's tax law revision that year would halve the minimum threshold from ₩30,000 to ₩15,000 with effect from the following year. The same report carried a second change arriving on the same date: the immediate refund limit doubling from ₩500,000 to ₩1,000,000 per purchase, and from ₩2,500,000 to ₩5,000,000 across a trip. The ₩30,000 on the airport page is the number that was replaced.

We could not settle it beyond argument, because the decree that fixes these amounts sits on the national law portal behind a page that will not render for us, and we do not print figures we have not read. So: two official sources, one announced change that explains the gap, and a practical consequence that does not depend on which is right. **If you have a receipt between ₩15,000 and ₩29,999, ask anyway.** The worst outcome is a no.

This is the third example we have collected of the same pattern — [the English page of a Korean service is usually not a translation but an older copy](/en/now/english-pages-korea-out-of-date/), and the number frozen in it is the number that costs you.

## The mistake happens at the check-in desk, not the refund counter

The airport sequence is counter-intuitive, and the order is the whole thing. Both the Korea Tourism Organization and the airport describe it the same way:

1. Check in and take your boarding pass — **but do not hand over your bag yet.**
2. Show the goods and the refund receipts to a customs officer and get the export approval stamp.
3. *Then* check the bag, at the oversized baggage counter.
4. Take passport and stamped receipts to a refund counter or kiosk. Cash comes after security, inside the duty free zone.

If the goods are going into checked baggage, the airport's instruction is to tell the airline staff so before the bag is tagged, then have the bag inspected and put it on the large belt beside the customs desk. The failure mode is quiet and total: the bag goes down the belt with the goods inside, and there is nothing left to show an officer.

Two things worth knowing before you plan the morning. The kiosks run 24 hours, but the staffed counters do not — Incheon lists roughly 07:00 to 19:00 at Terminal 1 and 07:00 to 21:00 at Terminal 2. And the airport says plainly that incomplete documents may be rejected. If you miss the counter, you can write your details on the receipt and drop it in the box; the money then goes to an international credit card, Alipay or WeChat Pay within one to two months.

## What to say

The tax does not come off because you are a foreigner. It comes off because you asked, at the moment you paid, with your passport in your hand. The passport is the part people forget — it is the document the till needs, not the one in the hotel safe.

At the counter, the sentence that works is short. **택스리펀 되나요?** *(taek-seu-ri-peon doe-na-yo)* — "does tax refund work here?" The borrowed word is the reliable one; it is the word on the sticker and the word staff use among themselves, and it belongs to the family of [English words that came into Korean and stayed](/en/decode/konglish-korean-words-that-look-english/). If you want to ask specifically for the tax to come off now rather than at the airport, that is **즉시환급 되나요?** *(jeuk-si-hwan-geup doe-na-yo)*.

Expect the answer to take a moment. In a Korean-language travel thread whose 84 comments we counted last month, four separate people described the same scene — staff at the till turning to each other to work out how the refund was done. It is not obstruction and it is not rare; the paperwork is unfamiliar to whoever is on shift. That sample skews toward well-prepared visitors who read Korean, which makes it the useful kind of evidence: if they waited, you will. Have the passport out, and let the queue behind you wait too.

And if the numbers on the receipt are what is slowing you down, the [two counting systems](/en/decode/korean-numbers-two-counting-systems/) article is the one that makes ₩15,000 sayable out loud.

We have not verified which specific shops are certified, which refund operator serves which store, or whether any particular category of goods is excluded. Nothing here is tax advice, and no page we read promises that a given purchase will be approved.
