Nobody at a Korean till asks how old you are. The question is 몇 년생이세요? *(myeot nyeon-saeng-i-se-yo)* — *what year were you born?* In a K-drama the same beat is blunter: 민증 *(min-jeung)*, hand it over. Both versions skip your age entirely and go straight for a year, and that is not an accident of phrasing. The law that decides who can buy soju does not care about your birthday either.

## The clause counts years, not birthdays

The statute is the 청소년 보호법 *(cheong-so-nyeon bo-ho-beop)* — the Youth Protection Act. It never states a drinking age. It defines who is a "youth", bans selling them alcohol and tobacco, and lets everyone else through. The definition is one sentence long, and the second half is the part English summaries drop:

Read the exception again. You stop being a "youth" not on your nineteenth birthday but on **1 January of the year your nineteenth birthday falls in**. For 2026 the arithmetic collapses to one question: were you born in 2007 or earlier? If yes, every bar and convenience store in the country may serve you — even if your actual birthday is still months away.

So "the drinking age in Korea is 19" is true in the middle and false at the edges. A visitor born in December 2007 is 18 by every passport in the world for almost all of 2026, and may legally buy soju in Seoul the entire year.

## Born a day apart, served a year apart

<ChartBirthYearGate caption="Three example birthdays run through the actual rule. The circles are nineteenth birthdays — the thing English guides assume matters. If this looks familiar, it is the system Korea supposedly abolished. [The 2023 age-unification law](/en/guides/korean-age-what-changed-in-2023/) made international age the default reading of every statute — *"unless a specific provision says otherwise."* This is the provision that says otherwise, and it is the one a traveller is most likely to collide with. The Act kept year-based counting deliberately: it makes a doorman's job possible. Nobody checking a card at midnight wants to compare month and day. Birth year alone decides, which is exactly why the question at the till asks for it.

The same definition governs cigarettes: the Act's banned-goods list names liquor under the Liquor Tax Act and tobacco under the Tobacco Business Act in the same breath, so the birth-year rule and the ID check are identical at the tobacco counter.

## Why the cashier will not just wave you through

The penalty structure explains the stubbornness. Selling alcohol or tobacco to a youth carries **up to two years' imprisonment or a ₩20,000,000 fine** under article 59 — and the penalty lands on the seller, not the buyer. Article 28 requires anyone selling these goods to verify the customer's age and identity, and the ban explicitly covers vending machines and unmanned shops, which is why a self-service kiosk shows you a 성인 인증 *(seong-in in-jeung)* — adult verification — screen before it shows you cigarettes.

Those screens usually verify through a Korean mobile number registered in your name — the same identity wall we mapped in [the 010-number article](/en/decode/korea-esim-no-phone-number/). At a staffed till the fallback is a human looking at a document. At an unstaffed one there may be no fallback at all.

## The word on the door is not in the dictionary

The document everyone means is the 주민등록증 *(ju-min-deung-nok-jeung)*, the resident registration card every Korean adult carries. In speech it clips to 민증 — spelled *min-jeung*, pronounced *min-jjeung*. The clipped form is real enough that the National Institute of Korean Language's open dictionary lists it, plainly glossed as "a shortened way of saying resident registration card" — yet the standard dictionary returns zero results for it. It lives in exactly the register where subtitles reach for "ID."

## What a door can demand, and what it can do if you refuse

For venues in the Act's youth-entry-banned category — the rooms built around drinking — checking is not a courtesy but a duty: article 29(2) orders the operator to verify age and keep youths out. Paragraph 4 then arms them:

Notice what the clause does and does not say. It names the resident registration card — including the mobile-app version Koreans can now show — and then leaves "any other document that proves age" open. It does not oblige a venue to accept any particular document, and it positively permits turning away anyone who shows nothing. For a visitor the document that proves your birth date is your passport, and whether a given door accepts a photo of it instead of the passport itself is that door's decision, backed by this clause. If a night out is the plan — [in Itaewon](/en/scenes/itaewon-alone-what-to-eat-buy-and-when-to-go/) or anywhere else — the physical passport is the version of you that argues back.

One more consequence of the penalty falling on the venue: being carded at 25 or 30 is routine, not an insult. The fine prints on the seller's side of the counter, so the seller checks.

## What this article does not claim

We read the Act's consolidated Korean text in force on 27 August 2026 (version effective 1 July 2026); parts of articles 28–29 were amended in April 2026 with effect from 8 October 2026, and the clauses quoted above carry earlier dates. Which specific bar or club categories fall under the entry ban is set by the Act's enforcement decree, which we did not map venue by venue. The Act does not publish a list of acceptable foreign documents, so we make no claim about what any particular venue will accept — only that it may refuse you if you present nothing.
