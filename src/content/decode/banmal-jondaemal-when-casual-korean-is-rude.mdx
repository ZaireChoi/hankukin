Look up **반말** *(ban-mal)* in the National Institute of Korean Language's own learner dictionary and you get two numbered senses. The first is a way of speaking used "when two people are very close, or their relationship does not clearly define who is the senior." The second is a way of speaking used "to talk down to a younger or inferior person."

Those are opposite social acts. The dictionary translates both of them into English as **informal speech**.

That collapse is not sloppiness — English simply has no separate word for either one. But it is the reason a whole class of moment goes past English-speaking visitors unregistered. Someone drops the polite endings when talking to you, and you have no way to tell from the words whether you have been let in or put down.

## Two entries, one English phrase

Here is the second sense in the original, because it is blunter in Korean than the gloss suggests:

The English there is the institute's own translation, not ours. Its example sentences for that second sense are about a clerk shouting at a company president and resigning, and about a junior student addressing a senior without warning. This is the register of insult.

Its opposite, **존댓말** *(jon-daen-mal)*, gets one sense and no such double life: "a form used in referring to a person or thing in order to convey respect." The dictionary lists 경어 and 높임말 as synonyms. Its illustrative dialogue is a grandfather correcting a child — not 밥 드세요 *(bap deu-se-yo)* for "please eat", but 진지 잡수세요 *(jin-ji jap-su-se-yo)*, swapping two ordinary words for their respectful counterparts.

So the pair is asymmetric in a way the English pair "informal / polite" is not. *Jondaemal* is one thing. *Banmal* is two, and only context separates them.

## The ladder underneath

Both words are informal labels for something Korean grammar handles precisely. The technical system is called 상대경어법 *(sang-dae-gyeong-eo-beop)* — listener honorifics — and it sorts every sentence ending into one of six levels, split into a formal set and an everyday set.

Two things in that diagram are worth slowing down on.

**해요체 *(hae-yo-che)* is in the everyday set, not the formal one — and it is still polite.** This surprises people who assume formality and politeness are the same dial. The encyclopaedia describes 해요체 as the level used to someone above you when you are not standing on ceremony, and also to equals or juniors when you want to treat them with respect. That combination is why it is the most widely used level in ordinary conversation today. 하십시오체 *(ha-sip-si-o-che)*, by contrast, is described as unsuitable for equals and juniors: it goes upward only.

For a visitor, that resolves into one instruction. **Put 요 on the end of everything.** It is the reason every phrase in [our list for shops and restaurants](/en/decode/korean-phrases-shops-restaurants/) ends that way, and it is close to impossible to give offence with it.

**Two of the six rungs have quietly emptied.** The encyclopaedia says of 하오체 that its use is not active in modern Korean, and it places 하오체 and 하게체 together among speakers of middle age and above — which is precisely why, it notes, the grading scheme is being re-examined. The consequence is real for anyone working through a textbook: the six-level table is accurate as a description of the language and misleading as a description of a week in Seoul. Four of the six will cover almost everything you hear.

## What decides which one

The encyclopaedia lists four measures for working out who ranks above whom: age, kinship, social position, and power. Then it says something a reference work rarely admits.

When the four measures conflict — an older person in a junior post, a customer younger than the shopkeeper — priority is settled case by case, and the encyclopaedia notes that the ordering shifts with the situation, the generation, the closeness of the two people and the region. Its own summary is that the decision is not easy.

That is worth carrying, because English-language explanations of Korean honorifics tend to arrive as tidy flowcharts. The tidiness is imported. Age comes first often enough that [Korean age arithmetic](/en/guides/korean-age-what-changed-in-2023/) used to be a genuine obstacle to starting a conversation — you could not choose your verb endings until you knew who was older, which is also why strangers ask.

## Korean has a word for the state itself

English can describe being on first-name terms. Korean has a noun for the specific condition of having dropped the honorifics with someone: **너나들이** *(neo-na-deu-ri)*. The Standard Korean Dictionary defines it as calling each other 너 *(neo)* and 나 *(na)* — "you" and "I" — and speaking without reserve, or the relationship in which people do so.

It is not alone. The encyclopaedia's closing section points out that Korean carries a small vocabulary about honorific use itself — verbs for raising someone, for lowering them, for treating them as a friend, for being on 너나들이 terms. Its inference is that ordinary speakers were conscious of this system long before grammarians got to it.

A language builds nouns for the states its speakers need to name. That there is a word for *this pair no longer uses honorifics with each other* tells you it is a status people track.

## The moment it changes, and what subtitles do with it

Because the level sits in the verb ending, every single sentence a character speaks declares a position. A switch from 해요체 to 해체 is therefore not a change of mood; it is a change of stated relationship, and it is audible to everyone in the room.

English subtitles cannot carry it. There is no ending to change. This is the same erasure that happens to [oppa, hyung, unnie and noona](/en/decode/oppa-hyung-unnie-noona/), and the two work together — a character moving from a title to a first name, and from 요 to no 요, is doing one thing twice.

What you can listen for, once you know the shape: whether sentences end in **-요** or **-습니다** or in a bare **-아 / -어**. You do not need to parse the sentence. The last syllable carries it.

The permission itself is usually asked out loud, in some version of 말 놓을까요? *(mal no-eul-kka-yo)* — shall we drop the formal speech. That phrase is not a headword in the Standard Korean Dictionary, which we read as meaning it is ordinary speech rather than a set formula. It is a real question, and it is asked because the answer is not automatic.

## If someone uses banmal on you

Sense one or sense two — that is the question, and it is answerable, but not from the verb ending alone.

Weigh three things. **Are they older than you, or in a service relationship with you?** An older shopkeeper using plain endings with a visibly younger stranger is common and is not aimed at you personally. **Did anything change?** Sense two frequently arrives as a switch mid-conversation, and the switch is the message. **What are you being called?** Korean pairs honorific endings with honorific titles. The dictionary's entry for 경칭 *(gyeong-ching)*, respectful forms of address, gives six examples: 님, 씨, 선생, 각하, 부인, 귀하. Two of those are worth a footnote — 각하 *(gak-ha)*, roughly "Your Excellency", is not in ordinary use today, and 부인 *(bu-in)* attaches only to women. A dictionary keeps entries after the street has stopped using them; treat the list as a range rather than as six live options. What matters for you is the shape: endings dropping while a title stays is a different event from both dropping at once.

And the reply that works in nearly every case is to keep using 요 yourself. It is not a rebuke and it costs nothing, but it declines the frame without saying anything about it — which is exactly how the system is designed to be used.

## What we checked, and what we did not

Every definition above comes from the National Institute of Korean Language — the Basic Korean Dictionary for 반말, 존댓말 and 경칭, and the Standard Korean Dictionary for 너나들이 — read on 18 August 2026. The English for the two senses of 반말 is the institute's own published translation, not ours. The six levels, their endings, and the statements about which are now confined to older speakers come from the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture of the Academy of Korean Studies, whose articles on 경어 and 공손법 were last revised on 7 February 2023.

Both encyclopaedia articles are signed academic contributions, and the encyclopaedia states plainly that they represent their authors' scholarly views rather than an institutional position. We have flagged in the text where the sources themselves report unsettled questions, which is most sharply the matter of what determines rank.

What we have not done is measure anything. We ran no survey of how often each level is used, by whom, or in which city, and the diagram's "everyday" and "rare" labels restate the encyclopaedia's characterisations rather than counting anything. Regional and generational variation is real and we have not mapped it. We also make no claim about usage in any particular film or television programme; we have described what the words do, not what any script does with them.
