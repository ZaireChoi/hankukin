You land at Incheon, scan a QR code you bought a week ago, and you have data before you reach immigration. No queue, no counter, no swapping a tiny piece of plastic and losing your own SIM in the process.

That part works, and it is genuinely better than what came before.

**Then, three days later, you try to order fried chicken to your hotel room at eleven at night and the app refuses to let you finish signing up.**

Nothing is broken. You just ran into the thing nobody mentions when they tell you to get an eSIM.

## An eSIM sells you data. It does not sell you a number

A tourist eSIM for Korea is **data-only**. You get an allowance of mobile data and nothing else — no Korean phone number, no ability to receive a text message, no voice line.

For most of a trip that is fine, because almost everything you need runs on data. Maps work. Translation works. KakaoTalk works, because it is an internet messenger. You can call home over FaceTime or WhatsApp.

**What does not work is anything that wants to send you a code.**

## Why Korea in particular

Korea leans on **본인확인** *(bon-in-hwag-in)*, identity verification, far more heavily than most countries. Signing up for a service that involves payment or delivery routinely means proving who you are, and the ordinary way of doing that is a text message to a phone number registered in your name with a Korean carrier.

A foreign SIM cannot be checked that way. A data-only eSIM has no number to check at all.

This is not aimed at visitors. It is a domestic system that visitors happen to run into, which is exactly why it catches people off guard — nothing warns you, and the app just stops.

**One admission before we go further.** We have not confirmed this against a primary Korean-language source — a regulator's page or a carrier's own documentation — and we usually would. What follows rests on published English guidance and on how the system is known to work. The structural claim we are confident in; the specific list below, less so.

## What actually breaks, in practice

**Food delivery.** Baemin, Coupang Eats and the rest tie accounts to a verified Korean number. This is the one that stings most, because it is the thing you want at eleven at night.

**Korean online shopping.** Coupang and similar will let you browse. Completing a purchase is a different matter.

**Some bookings.** Restaurants, tickets and services that take reservations often want a number to confirm — and a foreign number is sometimes rejected by the form itself, not by policy.

**Anything that texts you a code.** If a screen says a verification message has been sent, and you have a data-only eSIM, that message is going nowhere.

Take that list as **likely rather than certain.** Sign-up policies change without announcement and several of these services have tightened and loosened their handling of foreign accounts more than once. We checked published guidance on 16 August 2026 rather than attempting each sign-up ourselves.

## What does not break, and this is most of a trip

**Kakao T**, the taxi app, is the important one. It runs on data and accepts foreign cards, so you can hail a taxi without a Korean number. That single fact removes most of the anxiety people have about arriving without a local line.

Maps, transit times, translation, museum and palace information, and messaging all run on data.

**And Korea has unusually good public wi-fi** — subway stations, buses, cafés, most public buildings. Even if your data runs out you are rarely offline for long.

## So do you need a Korean number?

For a short trip of sightseeing, eating in restaurants you walk into, and getting around: **no.** Buy the eSIM, save the queue, enjoy the trip.

You want a Korean number if you plan to:

| | |
|---|---|
| Order delivery to your accommodation | The classic one |
| Buy from Korean online shops | Rather than carrying it home |
| Make reservations that need confirming | Some restaurants, some services |
| Stay long enough to need any ongoing service | Anything with an account |

If that is you, buy a **prepaid SIM with a number** rather than a data-only eSIM. These are sold at the airport and at some convenience stores, generally need only a passport, and the carrier gives you an 010 number.

**You can also do both**: eSIM for data now, a numbered SIM later if you find you need one. The eSIM does not have to be your only line.

## The practical order of operations

**Buy the eSIM before you fly.** You will get a QR code. Install it while you still have wi-fi at home — installation needs a connection, and doing it at the airport when you have neither Korean data nor a working foreign line is the one avoidable mistake here.

**Check your phone takes an eSIM.** Most phones from about 2019 onward do, but some regional models are excluded and some carrier-locked handsets refuse. Your phone's settings will say.

**Keep your own SIM in.** That is the whole point of an eSIM — your usual number stays reachable for messages from home.

We have deliberately not quoted prices or data allowances. They differ by product and by week, and a number that is wrong at a checkout screen is worse than no number at all.
