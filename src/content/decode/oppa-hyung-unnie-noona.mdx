Watch any Korean drama for ten minutes and you will notice that characters almost never say each other's names. They say **oppa**, **hyung**, **unnie**, **noona** — and the subtitle usually replaces all of it with a name, or deletes it.

Something is being lost there, and it is not decoration. In a lot of scenes it is the plot.

## The four words, and who says which

All four literally mean *older sibling*. Which one you use depends on **your own** gender, not only the other person's.

| You are | They are older, male | They are older, female |
|---|---|---|
| **Female** | 오빠 *oppa* | 언니 *unnie* |
| **Male** | 형 *hyung* | 누나 *noona* |

So a woman calling a man *oppa* and a man calling the same man *hyung* are saying the identical thing. If a female character suddenly uses *hyung*, that is not a slip — it is a signal, and the writer meant it.

There is no equivalent word for someone younger. You use their name. That asymmetry is the whole system in one line: **the words exist to mark the person above you.**

## They are not really about family

Here is the part that makes Korean unusual, and it is the point the encyclopaedia makes directly: kinship terms in Korean are used for **non-relatives** as much as relatives.

The woman calling a man *oppa* is usually not his sister. She may be his girlfriend, a colleague two years below him, a fan, or someone he met last week. The word has been borrowed out of the family and into ordinary life, and it took the family's hierarchy with it.

This is why the subtitle cannot win. Translate *oppa* as "brother" and you imply a family that does not exist. Translate it as his name and you delete the relationship the scene is establishing.

## What each one actually carries

**오빠 (oppa)** does the most work and causes the most confusion. Between a woman and an older man it can be neutral, affectionate, flirtatious or established-couple, depending entirely on tone and context. A scene where a woman switches from his name or from a formal title to *oppa* is a scene where something changed.

**형 (hyung)** between men is the workhorse. It marks seniority and, used warmly, loyalty. Male friendships in drama are frequently built and broken over whether one man will call the other *hyung*.

**언니 (unnie)** between women is the closest to plain warmth. It also travels furthest outside its literal meaning — customers use it with shop staff, and it appears in service contexts where no closeness exists at all.

**누나 (noona)** carries an extra layer, because a younger man calling an older woman *noona* is a recognised romantic configuration in Korean media, common enough to have its own genre label.

## The one you will hear at work and school

**선배 (seonbae)** is not a sibling word. It means someone senior to you in the same institution — school, company, industry — and its opposite, **후배 (hubae)**, means junior.

*Seonbae* is more formal than *oppa* or *unnie* and says nothing about closeness, only position. Watch for a character moving from *seonbae* to *oppa*: that is the relationship changing, stated without anyone stating it.

## Why Korean does this at all

Korean has no comfortable neutral "you." The pronoun exists, but using it with someone older is rude, and using it with a stranger is worse. This is also why [Korean age](/en/guides/korean-age-what-changed-in-2023/) mattered so much — you cannot pick the right word until you know who is older. So the language solves the problem by **naming the relationship instead of the person**.

Once you see that, a lot of dialogue reorganises itself. The characters are not avoiding names. They are constantly, quietly restating where they stand relative to each other — and the moment one of them changes the word, everyone in the room has heard it.

The encyclopaedia notes something else worth carrying: these terms vary by region and by family, and a woman may use different terms for her own family than for her husband's. There is no single flat rulebook, which is exactly why native speakers still argue about edge cases.

## Two things to listen for

**A switch mid-scene.** Name → *oppa*, or *seonbae* → *oppa*, or *oppa* → back to a formal title. Each of those is a plot beat.

**Refusal.** A character declining to call someone *hyung*, or insisting on a formal title, is refusing the relationship on offer. That refusal is often louder than anything said out loud.

## What this article does not claim

We are describing usage as documented by the Academy of Korean Studies and the National Institute of Korean Language. Actual usage varies by region, generation and family, and slang usage moves faster than any reference work.

We have also not attempted to cover the full kinship system, which extends to dozens of terms distinguishing paternal from maternal lines, older from younger siblings of parents, and relations by marriage. This page covers only the five terms you will hear most in contemporary drama.
