Type 속다 *(sok-da)* into the Jeju dictionary run by the island's own research centre and you get two numbered entries. Entry 01 is the verb every mainland Korean knows: to take a falsehood for the truth. Entry 02 is a different verb that happens to be spelled the same way: to put strength and effort into work. The standard-Korean gloss the dictionary attaches to entry 02 is 수고하다 *(su-go-ha-da)* — to take pains.

The Korean title of *When Life Gives You Tangerines* is built on entry 02. Almost everyone who reads it off a mainland dictionary gets entry 01. [We wrote about the island](/en/scenes/jeju-when-life-gives-you-tangerines/) and deliberately left the title unglossed, because we had not opened a Jeju dictionary and did not want to guess at a phrase in a language that is disappearing. We have opened it now. This is the piece we promised.

## The same five syllables, twice

The title is 폭싹 속았수다 *(pok-ssak sog-at-su-da)*. Split it into its three pieces and run each piece through the two dictionaries.

<ChartJejuSameShape caption="The left column is what a Seoul reader sees; the right is what the Center for Jeju Studies' dictionary says the same letters mean on the island. Nothing in the right column is a guess — entry numbers are the dictionary's own. What the two readings share is the intensifier at the front, and even that is spelled differently in each dictionary." />

The middle row is the one that does the damage. In the National Institute of Korean Language's Standard Korean Dictionary, 속다 has two senses, and both are about being fooled: 남의 거짓이나 꾀에 넘어가다 — to fall for someone's lie or scheme — and to mistake one thing for another. There is no entry about effort to catch you. So a mainland reader sees 속았수다, hears something close to 속았습니다 *(sog-at-seum-ni-da)*, and gets *you were deceived*. Read that way, the title of a warm family drama says *you were utterly taken in.*

The Jeju dictionary's second entry is not a metaphor or a stretch. It comes with its own conjugation table — 속곡, 속으난, 속앙 — and two example sentences, and the first of them is the title's own form.

The encyclopaedia's own explanation is that to labour and to suffer is to have one's insides rot — 속이 썩다 *(sog-i sseok-da)* is a living mainland idiom for exactly that feeling. So the island's word for hard work and the mainland's word for being fooled are not one word with two meanings. They are two words that ended up wearing the same clothes. The encyclopaedia names the greeting this produces, 속암수다 *(sog-am-su-da)*, and says outright that it gets mistaken for *I have been deceived*.

## The island's language centre disagrees with the title's spelling

Here is the thing we did not expect to find. The Center for Jeju Studies, which is the provincial body for the language, published a short video in 2025 on the title, and its written summary does not simply translate it. It corrects it.

<Original

  en="'Pokssak' is a non-standard form that comes from 'poksak'. In Jeju language it is 'boksak'."

  date="2026-09-05"

The same note says that 속았수다 means *you have worked hard* or *you have been through hardship*, and that the Jeju spelling is 속아수다 or 속앗수다 — not 속았수다. In other words, by the centre's own orthography, neither word of the title is written the Jeju way. The summary records that the spelling was argued over, back and forth, when the series aired.

We checked the dictionary against the note. 폭싹 returns no headword at all. 복삭 *(bok-sak)* returns seven, and sense 04 is 몹시 세차게, 또는 아주 심하게 — very forcefully, or extremely. That is the "so very" in *you have worked so very hard*. The mainland's 폭삭 *(pok-sak)*, by contrast, has eight senses in the Standard Korean Dictionary and every one of them is about something giving way: a heap collapsing, a body slumping, an egg gone off, a face aged overnight, a family ruined. None means "very". So the mainland misreading is not just *deceived*; the first word pushes it towards *ruinously deceived*.

None of this makes the title wrong as a title; it is the title. But if you want to write the phrase the way the island's own dictionary writes it, it is 복삭 속앗수다.

## The polite level with no 요 in it

The last syllables, -수다, matter more for a visitor than the rest, because they are the ones you will actually hear.

Standard Korean has a ladder of sentence endings — [we drew it](/en/decode/banmal-jondaemal-when-casual-korean-is-rude/) — and the two rungs a visitor learns to listen for are -요 *(yo)* and -습니다 *(seum-ni-da)*. Neither is the top rung on Jeju. The Digital Encyclopedia of Jeju Culture sets out three levels instead of six, and gives the same sentence at each:

| Level | Statement | Question | Command |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 라체 *(ra-che)* — plain | 밥 먹엇저 | 밥 먹언가? | 밥 먹으라 |
| 여체 *(yeo-che)* — middle | 밥 먹엄서 | 밥 먹어서? | 밥 먹어 |
| 서체 *(seo-che)* — polite | 밥 먹엄수다 | 밥 먹엄수강 | 밥 먹읍서 |

The polite row ends in -수다 for a statement, -수강 or -수까 for a question, and -읍서 for a request. So when an older islander says something to you that ends in 수다, they are being courteous, in the register they would use to a guest. Nothing about it is casual, even though it has none of the sounds a Seoul textbook told you to listen for.

For a visitor, the rule reduces to one line. On Jeju, **-수다 and -수까 are the polite endings.** Reply the way you would anywhere in Korea — with 요 on the end of everything, exactly as in [our phrases for shops and restaurants](/en/decode/korean-phrases-shops-restaurants/) — and you will be fine. You are not expected to produce the island's endings; you are only expected not to mistake them.

## An ending Seoul does not have

Now the part that is the actual grammar, and the reason the island's speech sounds to mainland ears like a different language rather than an accent.

Standard Korean marks "I am eating" with a separate construction, 먹고 있다 *(meok-go it-da)* — literally "eat and exist". Jeju puts the same information inside the verb. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, in an entry by the dialectologist Jung Seung-cheol, gives the pair:

<Original

  whoEn="Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, entry for Jeju dialect, Academy of Korean Studies"

The Jeju encyclopaedia describes the same two markers as aspect — completed versus in progress — and glosses the second with standard Korean's 고 있, offering 어디 감수까? *(eo-di gam-su-kka)*, "where are you going?", as the everyday example. Put the two accounts side by side and you have the shape of it: a single syllable, 암 or 엄, sitting inside the verb, which tells you the action is still happening. That is a job English gives to *-ing* and standard Korean gives to a whole extra word. We use *-ing* here as a comparison for readers, not as a claim about Korean grammar; the encyclopaedias call it tense or aspect, and we have kept to what they say.

This is why 속았수다 and 속암수다 are both real sentences and mean different things. With 앗, the effort is finished — *you have taken pains*, the thing said at the end of a day's work. With 암, it is still under way — *you are taking pains*, the greeting the Jeju encyclopaedia records. The title chose the finished form. That is a small piece of information about where the story stands, and it is invisible in *When Life Gives You Tangerines*.

## The vowel the mainland lost

One more thing sits inside that dictionary example above, and you may have noticed it: ᄆᆞᆫ. The second letter is not one of the vowels you learn in [a first pass at the alphabet](/en/hangul/learn-to-read-korean-signs-alphabet/). It is 아래아 *(a-rae-a)*, a vowel that standard Korean stopped writing and stopped saying. Jeju kept it. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture describes it as pronounced further back than 아 and between 오 and 아 in height, with the lips somewhat rounded, and it appears, as a rule, only in the first syllable of a word.

It is also going. The same entry reports that speakers under fifty — as of 2014 — mostly say 오 in its place, and that most young islanders now speak a variety of standard Korean rather than the traditional dialect, so that the biggest difference in Jeju speech today is not between the north and south of the island but between generations. The Jeju encyclopaedia says the same about the vowel. We looked for the UNESCO endangered-language classification you will find quoted in most English articles and could not open the original atlas entry, so we have not repeated it; the two encyclopaedias' descriptions of a language thinning by generation are what we can stand behind.

## Words you will actually meet

A handful of Jeju words leave the dictionary and turn up on signs, menus and in conversation. Each of these is a headword we opened.

**올레** *(ol-le)*. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture defines it as the short lane that runs from the alley into a house's yard. It is the same word you see on the island's walking-trail signs: a threshold, not a road.

**무사** *(mu-sa)*. Why, for what reason. It is a different word from the Sino-Korean 無事, "without incident", which the Jeju dictionary lists separately as 무ᄉᆞᄒᆞ다.

**하영** *(ha-yeong)*. A lot; beyond the usual measure. 하영 먹어수다, in the Jeju encyclopaedia's example, is "I ate a great deal", said politely.

**비바리** *(bi-ba-ri)*, an unmarried young woman; **구덕** *(gu-deok)*, the basket; **허벅** *(heo-beok)*, the water jar; **지실** *(ji-sil)*, a potato. The encyclopaedia lists all four among the island's own vocabulary. You will meet the last three as objects in museums and as names of restaurants.

## What we opened, and the one thing we did not

Every definition here comes from four public institutions, all read on 5 September 2026: the Jeju Dictionary and the education-platform archive of the Center for Jeju Studies; the Digital Encyclopedia of Jeju Culture and the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, both of the Academy of Korean Studies; and the Standard Korean Dictionary of the National Institute of Korean Language. The Jeju Dictionary labels itself a trial service. Both encyclopaedia entries are signed academic contributions, and the Academy states that they reflect their authors' views rather than an institutional position; where the two entries describe the same ending in different terms, we have shown both rather than picking one.

What we did not open is the UNESCO atlas. We also make no claim about how the phrase is spoken in the series itself, or by whom; we have described what the dictionaries say the words mean, not what any actor does with them.
