Here are two sentences about the same city, published by two arms of the same city government.

The **City of Seoul** publishes a fare table for the subway. We read it on 17 August 2026: the page was last revised on 11 July 2025 and the subway table itself is dated 28 June 2025. A single adult ride on a card is **₩1,550**. The same page works an example for the early-bird discount and prices a city bus at **₩1,500**.

The **Seoul Tourism Organization** publishes an English guidance page telling visitors how to top up the transit card that comes with the Discover Seoul Pass. It says this:

> Note : The basic subway fare is 1,250 KRW

How stale is 1,250? Seoul's own fare page settles it in a worked example about the early-bird discount, in Korean, in passing:

A city bus is ₩1,500. Not one Seoul fare is ₩1,250 any more.

Nobody is lying. The tourism organisation is not trying to mislead anyone; the number was simply true once, and the page has not been touched since. **That is the whole problem, and it is worth more of your attention than any single wrong figure.**

## The pattern

Visitors arrive expecting the English version of a Korean service to be the Korean version with the words swapped. It usually is not. Across the things we have checked this month, the foreigner-facing version turns out to be one of three things:

**Older.** The fare above. The page still says what it said before the fare changed.

**Smaller.** KT's English roaming page sells you a data eSIM online. The voice eSIM — the one with a phone number — is marked **"Available on site only."** You cannot buy it before you fly. The product exists; the online English path to it does not.

**Shaped differently.** SK Telecom will sell a voice eSIM online, but the passport verification that activates it runs **08:00–22:00 Korean time**. Land at 1am and the thing you bought does not work until morning. Nothing is broken. The counter is just closed, and the page you bought from is open all night.

None of these is a translation error. In every case the English page is accurate about something — it just is not the same something.

## Why we are writing this rather than a list of fixes

We counted where visitors actually get stuck. Across 107 comments in two long Korean-language threads written by people using foreign cards and phones in Korea, the recurring shape was not "I could not find the English version." It was **"I found the English version and it did not do what it said."**

That is a different problem, and it has a different remedy. You cannot solve it by looking harder for an English page. You solve it by knowing which kind of page you are on.

## Three questions that catch most of it

**Does the page carry a date?** Korean government pages usually do — look for 수정일 (revised) or a bracketed date like `(250628기준)` meaning "as of 28 June 2025". If the English page has no date and the Korean one does, the Korean one is the live copy and the English one is a snapshot of it. This is the single most useful habit on this page.

**Does the English page offer fewer options than the price list implies?** If a product page lists four plans and the English booking form shows two, the other two are not gone. They are somewhere else — usually a counter, usually at the airport.

**Does it say where, or only what?** "Available on site only" is doing a lot of work in that KT sentence. English pages tend to describe the product well and the *procedure* badly, because procedure is where the local assumptions live — the ones nobody writing in Korean has to state.

## Two live examples, checked today

**The Discover Seoul Pass transit card.** The English page carries the stale fare above. It also contains a genuinely important line that a lot of buyers miss:

> Card Pass has a Transportation Card function.
> ※ The Mobile Pass does not have it.

Buy the mobile version because it is convenient, expecting the transit card everyone mentions, and you will not have one. That is not a translation problem — it is stated plainly in English. It is a *reading* problem, and it exists because the sentence is buried under a heading about topping up.

**The eSIM that comes with the same pass.** Seoul Tourism Organization's English page is clear about several things: the eSIM is five days (120 hours), downloading it does not start your pass clock, and you can keep using it after the pass expires — but you must download it before the pass expires. Once you activate the pass, no refund.

What the page does **not** say is whether that eSIM carries a phone number or only data. We looked and could not find it stated. So we are not going to tell you either way. If the difference matters to you — and [it matters more than most people expect](/en/decode/korea-esim-no-phone-number/) — assume data only until someone official tells you otherwise.

## SRT, all the way to the payment button

We went through the English booking flow for SRT, the operator that runs Seoul–Busan out of Suseo. Four steps: Inquiry, Booking, Payment, Ticketing. **It works.** English station names, a real timetable with sold-out and available trains, a form that accepts a foreign card. Nobody is locked out.

What it has instead is a set of edges, and they are worth knowing before you are standing in one.

**You choose a class, not a seat.** The results table offers First and Standard. There is no seat map, no window-or-aisle. If you have a preference, the English flow does not ask for it.

**The password is a number.** The form wants one to hold your booking, and states the rule plainly:

> Password ※ 6~13 digit number.

Not a password in the sense you are used to. Digits only. Pick something you will still have at the station, because this is what identifies the booking.

**Typos are close to permanent.** Three lines sit above the form:

> If your information is incorrect, ticket exchange may be denied.
> Once saved, it cannot be edited. Please verify your details.

Your name must be in English and contain no spaces — the same rule applies to the email field. Given that an incorrect entry can block an exchange and cannot be corrected afterwards, this is the one screen on the trip worth reading twice.

**And the trap.** Once you are on the page headed **Foreigner**, the payment section offers two tabs — **Domestic Card** and **Overseas Card** — and the one already selected is **Domestic Card**.

That is the pattern of this whole article in a single default. The page knows you are a foreign customer; it says so in its own heading. The tab underneath is still set to the card you almost certainly do not have.

**One thing to decide before you start.** The consent screen states what SR collects for a foreign booking — name, email address, credit card — and that it keeps them for **five years**, citing the E-Commerce Consumer Protection Act. Consent is required; the page notes that reservation and payment for non-registered customers may be restricted without it. That may be entirely reasonable to you. It should not be a surprise at step two.

**What we did not see.** The nationality field is a required dropdown and we did not open it, so we cannot confirm what it does or does not list. We did not complete a purchase, so the final Ticketing step is unverified. And we have not compared this with the Korean-language flow, so we are not claiming the Korean side offers seat selection — only that the English side does not.

## What this costs, concretely

A wrong fare of 300 won is not the damage. The damage is what you build on top of it.

You budget your week from the 1,250 figure and top up too little, then find the machine at the gate at rush hour. You read that the pass includes a transit card, buy the mobile one, and arrive at the turnstile with nothing to tap. You buy an eSIM at 11pm from a page that never closes, and cannot make a call until 8am.

Each is small. Each happens at the exact moment you have no slack — arrival, a gate, a queue.

## What we do about it here

Every article on this site carries the date its facts were checked and links the Korean page they came from, so you can see the original sentence rather than trust our reading of it. Where a number matters, we print the Korean line next to the English one. That is not a stylistic choice — it exists because of the pattern this article is about.

And when we cannot verify something, we say so instead of filling the gap. There are four such holes in this piece: whether the Discover Seoul Pass eSIM carries a phone number, what SRT's nationality dropdown lists, what its final Ticketing step does, and whether the tourism organisation's Korean-language pages carry the same stale fare. None is confirmed, and none is guessed at above.

---

**Two lines to keep.**

If a Korean page and its English page disagree, **the Korean one is current.** Not because it is more official — because it is the one somebody has a reason to update.

And when a Korean service does build you a foreigner path, **check its defaults before you trust them.** The page can know exactly who you are and still be pre-set for somebody else.
