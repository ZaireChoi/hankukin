If you have read anything in English about getting around Seoul in the last two years, you have been told to buy the Climate Card — 62,000 won, thirty days, unlimited subway and buses. It is good advice. It is about to become wrong advice.

**Seoul stops selling the 30-day top-up on 31 August 2026. The last day you can ride on a prepaid one is 29 September — 30 September if yours is the post-paid kind. On 1 October the 30-day pass is withdrawn.**

The short 1-to-7-day passes are not going anywhere. That distinction is the whole article.

That is not a rumour or a proposal. It is a table on Seoul Metropolitan Government's own transport site, with dates.

Seoul's stated reason is consolidation, not failure: the national government launched its own unlimited-fare product in January 2026 at the same price point, and running two identical schemes side by side confused people.

**Two conditions on that last day, easy to miss.** Normally, topping up lets you pick a start date within five days *counting the day you pay* — so up to four days later. A pass topped up on 31 August cannot defer its start at all. And Seoul adds that if your current pass expires on or after 5 September, you are not eligible for the 31 August final top-up in the first place.

For a visitor this was always the better product. Seoul does not publish a break-even figure and we are not going to invent one; work it against [what a subway, bus and taxi ride actually costs](/en/now/seoul-transport-fares-subway-bus-taxi/) and decide for your own itinerary. What is certain is that a two-week trip never needed a thirty-day pass.

The catch, in Seoul's words: **short-term passes cannot be charged in advance.** The clock starts on the day you top up, and a 1-day pass dies with that day's last train — not twenty-four hours later. Buy it on the morning you intend to use it, not the night you land.

Short passes also do not include Ttareungi (the city bike share) or the Han River bus. The 30-day pass had versions that did. That difference disappears along with the 30-day pass.

**And the short pass covers a smaller map.** The 30-day pass reached into seven Gyeonggi cities on Seoul's edge — Gimpo, Goyang, Gwacheon, Seongnam, Hanam, Namyangju and Guri. The short passes do not. Seoul puts the scope in a parenthesis, under the closure table:

## What the pass does not cover — and the airport problem

This trips up more visitors than the end date will. The Climate Card covers Seoul's subway, Seoul-licensed city and neighbourhood buses, and — on the 30-day pass only — some sections past the city border. It does **not** cover:

- Shinbundang Line, GTX
- Express buses, airport buses, buses licensed by other provinces
- Taxis

**Incheon Airport is a special case, and it works in one direction only.** On the Airport Railroad the ordinary coverage runs Gimpo Airport to Seoul Station. But Seoul lists an explicit exception: if you board inside the covered zone, you *may* tag out at Incheon Airport Terminal 1 or 2. You may not board there.

So the pass can take you **to** your departure flight, and cannot bring you **in** from your arrival one. Landing at Incheon, you are paying separately to reach the city — [the train, bus and taxi options are here](/en/now/incheon-airport-to-seoul-train-bus-taxi/).

And the failure mode is unforgiving. Tap in inside the zone, ride out of it, and you cannot tap out:

Seoul then spells out what happens instead: you call a station attendant at the exit, and pay the fare for the whole journey from where you boarded. It is also logged against you as a missed tap-out.

There is a small mercy for short-pass holders. Miss two tap-outs on a 30-day pass and the card is suspended for twenty-four hours. Seoul's usage page states the penalty does not apply to short-term passes.

## The refund rule nobody tells foreigners

This is the sentence we would put on a poster at the arrivals hall. It sits in a table cell on Seoul's usage page, in Korean, with no English equivalent we could find:

Read that against how refunds normally work. Seoul offers two routes: a transfer to a Korean bank account within five days, or — if you topped up with a credit or debit card — a partial cancellation of that original charge after identity verification. The card-cancellation route may well be open to a visitor. **The bank-transfer route is not, and that is the one the cash-at-head-office rule is about.**

Four more things about refunds, all from the same page:

- **There is a deadline.** Refunds are only for balances on a pass that has not yet expired; once the period is over, Seoul says no refund at all. For a physical card you have until 15 days after the expiry date to file.
- A 500-won fee comes off whatever is left.
- The 3,000-won card itself is not refunded, only the balance on it — the exception is a faulty card.
- You must register the card at `pay.tmoney.co.kr` first. Seoul words this as a requirement — "physical card users **must** register" — and then states the consequence of not doing it: **"without registration, refunds and the bike share are unavailable."** Being precise about what Seoul does and does not say: the only penalties it names are those two. It does not say an unregistered card will not open a gate. We are reading that as *registration gates your money, not your ride*, and we are telling you it is a reading, not a quotation.

That distinction resolves something we had recorded as contradictory testimony. In our comment tally, one traveller reported giving up on T-money registration entirely after a security-plugin install loop and getting a refund instead; another said topping up with cash at a convenience store was no trouble at all. Both are accurate — they were doing different things.

The practical conclusion for a short trip: buy the number of days you will actually use, and plan to walk away from whatever is left rather than budget a trip to a company office for a few thousand won.

## Buying the physical card

An iPhone changes the answer here. Seoul's usage page routes Android users to the mobile card and iPhone users to the physical card — the mobile Climate Card is Android only, and Seoul adds OS 12 or later, with some handsets unsupported.

The physical card costs 3,000 won. Seoul lists two places to buy it: the newer ticket machines inside stations — **line 1 only between Seoul Station and Cheongnyangni**, plus lines 2 to 8 — and convenience stores near stations. Cash or a credit/debit card both work; corporate cards are refused.

Topping up is a wider list than buying. The in-station top-up machines are on line 1 (Seoul Station–Cheongnyangni), lines 2 to 9, the Ui-Sinseol, Sillim and Gimpo Gold lines. And since 25 November 2025 you can top up a physical card by holding it against the back of a phone running the Mobile T-money app — Seoul's press release says both Android and iPhone, paying by credit or debit card or open banking.

## What replaces it, and what we are not going to tell you

From 1 September 2026 Seoul directs everyone to the national card — 모두의 카드, with a Seoul-flavoured tier called 기후동행패스 launching the same day. Seoul answers the structural questions itself, but hands seven of the closure FAQ's thirty-one answers — fares, coverage, eligibility, how to sign up — straight to the K-pass helpline. Those are precisely the ones a visitor would ask.

We tried to check whether a short-stay visitor can register for it. **The K-pass site would not load for us, and we are not going to guess at a registration requirement we could not read with our own eyes.** If you are staying long enough for this to matter, that is a question for the K-pass helpline on 031-427-4415, not for a travel site.

There is also a wrinkle worth knowing if you go looking yourself: Seoul's older Climate Card FAQ page still lists only the 62,000 and 65,000-won options and an out-of-date list of subway sections, while the usage page — revised 8 June 2026 — carries the short passes, the Han River bus and the payback event. **The two official pages disagree.** We used the more recently revised one and the closure FAQ, and we have linked both so you can see the discrepancy for yourself.

## If you would rather not buy a pass at all

For a trip with a handful of subway rides, Seoul still sells a single-journey ticket at station machines: the fare plus a **500-won deposit**, and you get the deposit back from a refund machine after you exit. No account, no registration, no refund paperwork. It is not glamorous, and for four rides across three days it is entirely rational.

---

**Bottom line.** If you are travelling to Seoul in October 2026 or later, the 62,000-won 30-day Climate Card is gone and every guide still recommending it is out of date. If you are travelling before then, it works — a prepaid one until 29 September, a post-paid one until 30 September — but a 1-to-7-day pass almost certainly suits your trip better, costs less, carries no missed-tap penalty, and will still be on sale afterwards. Whatever you buy, file any refund before the pass expires, and do not count on a bank transfer unless you hold a Korean account.
