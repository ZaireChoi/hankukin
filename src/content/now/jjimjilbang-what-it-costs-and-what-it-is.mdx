Every English guide gives you the same sentence. *A jjimjilbang is a 24-hour Korean bathhouse where you can sleep for about ₩10,000.*

Each part of that is true somewhere in Korea and false somewhere else in Korea, and the word does not tell you which one you are walking into.

## What the law calls it

Start with the definition, because it is more interesting than the guides make it sound. Korea's Public Health Control Act defines the business — 목욕장업 — in two limbs.

A national statute names three specific minerals. The first limb of the same clause covers plain washing in water; this second limb is the jjimjilbang, and it is defined entirely as **a place that makes you sweat.**

Now read the clause immediately above it. **숙박업** — the lodging business — is defined as services letting a guest *sleep and stay*. A jjimjilbang is not that. It is licensed as a bathhouse, and sleeping is something the country does there anyway rather than something the licence is for.

That distinction is not pedantry. It is why there is no check-in, no room that locks, and no obligation to have a bed free when you arrive.

## One word, seven times the price

<ChartJjimjilbangPrices caption="What the same English word covers. Green: the Korea Consumer Agency's 참가격 survey of 목욕료 (bath entry) for June 2026, read 14 August 2026 — cheapest region ₩8,308, Seoul ₩11,000. Orange: one large operator's own published rates, read 22 August 2026. The government survey has no jjimjilbang line at all, so the two colours are measured on the same ruler but are not the same product." />

The government does run a price survey, and it is a good one — the Korea Consumer Agency publishes **목욕료**, the entry price for a public bath, for every province every month. In the June 2026 release that is **₩11,000 in Seoul** and **₩8,308** in the cheapest region.

**But the survey has no line for a jjimjilbang.** Its personal-services items are laundry, lodging, barber, salon and bath — and nothing else. This is why every English guide quotes a range with no source attached: there is no official figure to attach.

What there is instead is each operator's own published rate. One large chain lists **₩26,000** for the sweat-spa, ₩48,000 for its water park, and ₩58,000 for a pass covering both.

So the honest version of the sentence is: **a neighbourhood bath is under ₩11,000 and is a real thing to do; a resort spa is two to five times that.** Both get called a jjimjilbang in English.

## The two rules that catch people

From the same operator's published information, read on 22 August 2026:

| | |
|---|---|
| Sweat-spa opening hours | **10:00 – 22:00** |
| Included time | **6 hours** |
| After that | **₩5,000 per hour** |

**It closes at ten at night.** The "24-hour" line in the guides describes one kind of establishment — the older, cheaper, neighbourhood kind — and does not describe the modern resort spa at all. If you arrive at nine in the evening planning to stay the night, you have about an hour.

**And the ticket is not open-ended.** Six hours, then metered. Most bathing places in the world charge you once and let you drift; this one has a clock.

We are not presenting either figure as national. They are one operator's rules, on one date, and we are naming the operator so you can check them. The point is not the numbers — it is that **the numbers exist and vary**, which the single English word hides.

## When Koreans actually go

Here is the part that changes how you should schedule it.

Naver publishes search interest for any keyword by month. For **찜질방** across the last thirteen months, with the peak month set to 100:

| | |
|---|---|
| **January** | **100** |
| December | 79 |
| February | 76 |
| October | 62 |
| **June** | **32** |

**January runs three times June.** This is a winter thing. Koreans do not think of it as a spa treatment available all year; they think of it as what you do when it is cold outside.

On the same scale, **사우나** peaks at 48 — the jjimjilbang is more than twice the search interest of the plain sauna. And **세신**, the vigorous scrub-down that foreign visitors talk about most, never rises above 4.4. Even Koreans are not searching for it.

That seasonality lines up with something we wrote earlier: a Korean winter is a season of [moving between heated rooms](/en/now/when-does-it-snow-in-korea/), because the temperature hovers near freezing rather than sitting far below it. The bathhouse is not an escape from the winter. It is the shape of the winter.

## Going in the afternoon

Most visitors already have a hotel. That makes the sleeping question irrelevant and the daytime question the real one — and the daytime answer is good.

At Seoul's surveyed price of **₩11,000**, a public bath costs about the same as [a bowl of naengmyeon](/en/now/korea-food-prices-what-meals-cost/) and slightly more than [seven subway rides](/en/now/seoul-transport-fares-subway-bus-taxi/). For that you get two or three hours indoors on a cold afternoon, in a building where nobody is hurrying you and nothing needs to be booked.

And the building is not only heated rooms. The photographs on this page are from two ordinary establishments in and near Wonju, and what is in them is a comic library, a row of massage chairs, an arcade, and a snack counter.

Two structural things worth knowing before the door:

**The bathing floor and the sweating floor are different places with different rules.** The baths are single-sex and unclothed — that is what the law's first limb describes, and the regulations require men's and women's changing and bathing rooms to be run separately. The sweating rooms are the other limb: everyone is in the same cotton uniform, and they are mixed.

**You wash before you get in the water, not in it.** This is not etiquette advice invented for tourists; it is how the room is laid out. The showers face the wall and the tubs are for sitting in afterwards.

## What we could not find out

This article is thinner than we would like, and we would rather show you the holes than paper over them.

**We have no temperatures.** We wanted to tell you that one room is 50°C and another is 80°C, because that is the thing a first-time visitor actually wants. Operators publish room temperatures inside photographs, not as text, and we do not print numbers we have read off a picture.

**We have no order of operations from a source we trust.** Bath first or sweat first, and how long to stay in — every blog has an answer and none of them cite anything. We are not going to add a sixth version.

**We could not confirm the youth curfew.** It is widely reported that under-19s may not enter between 22:00 and 05:00 without a guardian, and we believe it is real, but the clause sits in an annexe to the enforcement rule that is published as a downloadable file rather than as readable text. Until we have opened it, we are not stating it as law.

**We could not confirm anything about tattoos.** Some establishments refuse entry to visibly tattooed customers. Whether that is regulation or house policy, we could not establish, and for a visitor with tattoos it is the single most useful thing on this page — so its absence is a real gap, not a rounding error.

**Every price here is one reading on one date.** The Consumer Agency figure is the June 2026 release; the operator's rates were read on 22 August 2026 and that operator runs different rates in high season. Check before you rely on either.

**The photographs are somebody else's, from 2020, of two specific places.** Korea's tourism board photographs parks, palaces and markets; its collection had no bathhouse in it at all, because these are private businesses. The pictures here came instead from Wikimedia Commons, released to the public domain by their photographer, and they show two ordinary establishments in and near Wonju in 2020. **They are not the place you will walk into**, and a resort spa charging ₩26,000 looks nothing like them.

**One thing the photographs settle, though.** Look at the room with the salt floor, and at the low door into the domed kiln. Whatever you were picturing when you read "Korean sauna", it was probably not that.
