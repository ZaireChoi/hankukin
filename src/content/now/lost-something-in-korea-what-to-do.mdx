Seoul's subway picked up **101,523 lost items in one year** — about 278 a day. Wallets were the single biggest category at 24,737, then phones and valuables at 20,131, then bags at 14,785.

**Two-thirds went home.** Seoul Transportation Corporation returned 66,426 of them to their owners.

So the odds are better than the sinking feeling in your stomach suggests. What defeats visitors is not that the system fails. It is that **the system moves**, and nobody tells you the timetable.

## The seven-day clock

This is the whole article. Seoul publishes the handling chain plainly:

| Where | How long |
| --- | --- |
| **The station** | Registered on the police portal the moment it is found |
| **The transit lost-and-found centre** | **7 days** |
| **The police station** | **9 months** |
| After that | Forfeited to the state, or donated |

**Which door you knock on depends on what day it is.** Lost it this morning? A subway lost-and-found centre. Lost it ten days ago? It is at a police station and the subway cannot help you. Most visitors discover this by going to the wrong one first, on a day they do not have spare.

## Before anything else: your transit card

This is the part almost nobody knows, and it is the reason to act today rather than tomorrow.

If you paid with a transit card, **show that card to a station attendant.** Seoul's own guidance explains why:

You almost certainly cannot describe your train. You do not know the car number, and "the 3 o'clock-ish one, greenish line" is not a search. **The card knows.** It converts a vague memory into a specific train, and staff search from there.

So the order is: **tell a station attendant the place and the time, and hand over the card you tapped with.** Not an email. Not a website. The attendant, at a station, with the card.

## The four centres, and which lines they cover

Seoul Transportation Corporation runs four lost-property centres. They are split by line, so going to the nearest one is the wrong instinct — go to the one that owns **the line you were on**.

| Lines | Centre | Station | Where inside |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · 2 | Sicheong | **City Hall** (Line 2) | B2 |
| 3 · 4 | Chungmuro | **Chungmuro** (Line 4) | B2 |
| 5 · 8 | Wangsimni | **Wangsimni** (Line 5) | B1 |
| 6 · 7 | Taereung | **Taereung Ipgu** (Line 7) | B3 |

**Bring photo ID.** You must prove you are the owner. For a visitor that means your passport.

## And the part that catches everyone: not all of it is one company

The four centres above cover the lines Seoul Transportation Corporation actually operates. Several stretches of the "Seoul subway" belong to somebody else, and they keep their own lost property:

- **Line 1 outside Seoul Station–Cheongnyangni** — Korail. Held at individual stations, then passed to the police.
- **Line 7, Kkachiul–Seongnam stretch** — Incheon Transit Corporation, at Incheon City Hall station.
- **Line 9** — Seoul Metro Line 9, at Dongjak station.

You rode one map. You did not ride one company. This is the same shape as [the English page that is not the Korean page](/en/now/english-pages-korea-out-of-date/) — a thing that presents as single and is administratively several.

## If it fell onto the tracks

Small things go over the platform edge constantly — phones, earbuds, cards. Seoul recorded 1,168 of these in a year, mostly phones.

**Staff will not open the platform screen doors for you.** Not for an urgent flight, not for anything. Track retrieval happens after the last train, at night, by staff following a procedure. Report it, then come back or arrange collection. Being upset at the attendant does not move this rule, and it is a safety rule rather than a bureaucratic one.

## After seven days: the police

Once it leaves the transit centre, the search moves to the national police lost-property system, **lost112**, which also has a mobile app. Station staff register every item they find on the police portal at the point of collection, so an item that has moved on is still findable — you are just searching a different database and collecting from a different building.

The legal backdrop, which explains why the police end up holding your wallet at all: under Korea's Lost Article Act, anyone who finds property must hand it to the police. That is not a courtesy. Seoul's own page notes that keeping found property without reporting it is an offence.

**One honest gap.** We could not open lost112 from outside Korea to check what it offers in English, so we are not going to describe its interface or promise you it has an English mode. If you are already in Korea, open it and see. If you are not, the phone numbers below work regardless of what the website does.

## What actually helps, from the people who do this daily

The staff at the centres say two things that are worth more than any procedure.

**Most items are found in the seat gap and on the luggage rack.** A single glance at your seat and the rack above it before you stand up removes most of this problem.

**Phones and wallets almost always go home; other things often do not** — not because nobody found them, but because there is no way to identify the owner. A business card tucked inside a bag is the difference between a return and a donation.

---

**In order, if it just happened.** Go to a station attendant. Say where and roughly when. Hand over the transit card you tapped with. If it is not found the same day, go to the lost-property centre for that line, with your passport, within a week. After a week, search lost112 and expect the collection point to be a police station, not a subway station.

## What this article does not claim

**The volume figures are from 2021.** Seoul published 101,523 items, 278 a day, a 65% return rate and the category breakdown for the year to 31 December 2021, in a report dated January 2022. We are quoting them as an order of magnitude, not as this year's number, and we could not find a more recent published breakdown.

**We have not verified current opening hours for the four centres**, only their locations, floors and phone numbers as published by Seoul. Call before you travel across the city.

**We could not reach lost112 from outside Korea** and therefore cannot describe its English support, its search fields or its app. That is a gap, and it is the one most likely to matter to a visitor. We would rather say so than guess.

**The seven-day and nine-month periods are as Seoul publishes them** for the transit handling chain. Individual cases vary — perishables, documents and high-value items can follow different rules under the Lost Article Act, and we have not read the Act's implementing decree line by line.
