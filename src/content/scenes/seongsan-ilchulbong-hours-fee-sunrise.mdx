This article went up on Monday, 7 September 2026. Anyone who set an alarm for four that morning,
paid for a taxi across the island and stood at the ticket window in the dark would have found it
shut. Not because of weather. Because it was the first Monday of the month.

That is the rule, and it is the single most expensive thing to not know about Jeju's most visited
natural landmark. It is printed on the operator's own page, in Korean, inside a tab that most
English write-ups never open — which is why so many of them describe Seongsan Ilchulbong
*(seong-san il-chul-bong)* as open every day of the year.

## The gate opens before the sun, but not by much

The office runs three sets of hours, and it also closes the ticket window a full hour before the
site itself shuts. That second number is the one that catches people arriving in the afternoon.

| Season | Open | Last tickets sold |
|---|---|---|
| May – August | 04:30 – 20:00 | 19:00 |
| March – April, September – October | 05:00 – 19:00 | 18:00 |
| January – February, November – December | 06:00 – 18:00 | 17:00 |

The same page carries one more line, in a footnote, that no English summary we have seen
reproduces. It means the table above is a plan and not a promise — **the sign posted at the gate
outranks anything printed here, including this article.**

Now put those opening times against the sunrise. The same office publishes a sunrise table, and it
is not a Jeju City table borrowed from the weather service — the footer says it is measured at the
peak itself. That is unusual enough to be worth the trip through a Korean-only page.

The last days of April are the tightest window in the whole published year: the gate opens
46 minutes before the sun does. And here is the honest gap in this article — **the operator does
not publish how long the climb takes.** Popular sources say anything from fifteen minutes to an
hour, which is another way of saying nobody has measured it for you. So we are not printing a
number. Treat the whole gap as the budget, be first through the gate, and if you are there in late
April do not stop for photographs on the way up.

## August is missing from the sunrise table

We opened all twelve months of 2026 on that page. This is what is actually there:

| Month (2026) | Sunrise times published |
|---|---|
| January – March | none |
| April – July | yes |
| **August** | **none** |
| September | yes |
| October – December | none |

October 2025 was empty too, so this is not a table that fills in behind you. Two things follow.
The first is that a visitor planning for autumn foliage or a New Year sunrise — the two occasions
Koreans actually come here for, and the site holds a sunrise festival on 31 December — cannot get a
time from the office that runs the place. The second is smaller and stranger: **August, the busiest
travel month in Korea, is the one blank month sitting inside an otherwise continuous run.**

We have no explanation for it and we are not inventing one. We are telling you it is blank so that
you do not go looking for a number that is not there.

## Who counts as an adult

| Ticket | Individual | Group of 10+ | Applies to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adult | ₩5,000 | ₩4,000 | 25 to 64 |
| Youth or serving soldier | ₩2,500 | ₩2,000 | 13 to 24 |
| Child | ₩2,500 | ₩2,000 | 7 to 12 |

Parking is free. Those were the fees on the operator's page on 7 September 2026; admission prices
in Korea are revised without much warning, so if you are reading this a season later, budget
loosely. Now read the right-hand column again: an adult here starts at **25**, not 19 and not 18.
A 24-year-old pays the youth fare, which is the opposite of what almost every English page about
Korean admission prices will have trained you to expect.

The table has no row for children under 7 and no row for visitors 65 and over. Both are very
probably free, and we are not going to write that they are, because the operator has not.
The number on the page is 064-710-7923.

## The postcard is a view of the cone, not from it

Something we noticed while checking the photographs for this article, and it changes what the
₩5,000 is for. Of the four official images of Seongsan Ilchulbong we looked at, three were taken
from somewhere else — the shore, the road, a viewing frame across the bay. Only the aerial shows
what you actually buy: the green bowl inside the crater.

So decide which one you came for. The silhouette that appears in every Jeju photograph is a picture
*of* Ilchulbong, taken from across the water, and you cannot take it from the summit. The ticket
buys the crater, the ash layers in the cliff, and the height. If you have someone in the group who
would rather not climb a sea-cliff staircase, the shore is not a consolation prize — it is where
the famous view is.

## Why you cannot do this from Jeju City

The office publishes its own transit times: about 70 minutes from Jeju International Airport,
about 68 from Jungmun, about 57 from Seogwipo, all along Route 1132, alighting at Seongsan.

Those are daytime journeys. Run the arithmetic for September and you need to leave Jeju City at
about 03:45 to be at the gate at 05:00. **We could not confirm that any bus runs at that hour.**
The Jeju bus information system gave us the stops and the routes but would not return first
departures or fares, so we are not printing either. The conclusion does not depend on the missing
number, though: the sunrise at Sunrise Peak belongs to people who slept in Seongsan. Book the
village, not the city.

If you are not driving — and Korea does not accept every foreign licence, which we covered in
[our guide to the island behind *When Life Gives You Tangerines*](/en/scenes/jeju-when-life-gives-you-tangerines/)
— an organised day tour solves the daylight problem and not the sunrise one. It arrives when
everyone else does.

One last thing, for the ride over. The village name and the peak name are standard Korean, but
almost nothing else you hear on that bus will be, and
[Jeju's own language is far enough from Seoul Korean](/en/decode/jeju-dialect-pokssak-sogatsuda-meaning/)
that mainland Koreans need subtitles too.

Everything above — hours, closed days, fees, sunrise times, transit times — comes from two pages
run by the office that operates the site, read on 7 September 2026. Where we have written that
something is missing, it is missing from those pages, and we have not filled the hole from
anywhere else.
