Two thousand won. That is the entire admission charge at the centre of this article, and almost
everyone who arrives in Suwon for *Lovely Runner* pays it in the wrong place, at the wrong time, or
does not need to pay it at all.

The problem is the names. **수원화성** *(Su-won Hwa-seong)* is the fortress — five and a half
kilometres of wall with four gates, wrapped around the old town. **화성행궁** *(Hwa-seong
Haeng-gung)* is the palace inside it. In English both get flattened to "Hwaseong," and the ticket
rules for the two are not similar at all.

## One of these charges you and one of them does not

The wall is not a ticketed attraction. It has no gate, no counter and no closing time, and the
operator says so in one line:

<Original
  ko="수원화성은 개방형이며 야간 관람 가능"
  en="Suwon Hwaseong is open-plan, and night viewing is possible."

You can walk onto the rampart at two in the morning and nobody will stop you. The fee table on the
same page — the one that says ₩2,000 — applies to the palace and to Hwaryeongjeon, the shrine beside
it. Nothing else.

| **Suwon Hwaseong** (the wall) | Always open, year-round | Free | Free | Free |
| **Hwaseong Haenggung** (the palace) | 09:00–18:00, year-round | ₩2,000 | ₩1,500 | ₩1,000 |

Checked against the operator's page on 2026-08-17.

Three things in that table cost people a day. **Last entry at the palace is 17:00**, a full hour
before the stated closing time. **The trolley leaves from Yeonmudae**, on the far east side of the
circuit, not from the palace where most visitors start. And the combined ticket only pays for itself
if you actually want two museums — at ₩4,000 it is double the palace alone.

## Where the drama actually was

*Lovely Runner* was filmed around **행궁동** *(Haeng-gung-dong)*, the neighbourhood inside the
walls, and specifically along the lanes near **화홍문** *(Hwa-hong-mun)* — the water gate at the top
of this page, where the wall crosses the Suwoncheon stream. Kyunghyang Shinmun, reporting from the
area, places the romance scenes on that stretch: the water gate, the stepping stones across the
stream, and **방화수류정** *(Bang-hwa-su-ryu-jeong)*, the pavilion just above it.

The same report identifies the two spots people actually queue for. Hwahong Mart, a corner shop on
the culture street, gave the crew the run of the store and now has stills from the episodes pinned
up outside. And the house where Im Sol lived at nineteen is now a café.

We are grading this as reported rather than official. The production filmed openly enough that local
traders described it to a reporter on the record, and the operator's tourism site keeps a section
called **TV속 수원화성** — "Suwon Hwaseong on TV" — though it points off to a blog rather than to a
filming register we could check. What we did not find is a statement from the production company
naming these addresses. So we are not going to write one.

## Someone lives behind the blue gate

The house used for Ryu Sun-jae, opposite the café, is a private home with residents in it. The
newspaper that reported the location said so in the same breath as reporting it, which is unusual
and worth repeating: this is a lane where people take their bins out.

Photograph the gate from the street, keep your voice down, and do not go up to it. Fans arrive here
with yellow umbrellas — that part is a genuine local custom now, and it is fine. Knocking is not.

## The exemptions, and the one we could not pin down

You do not have to pay the ₩2,000 at all if you turn up in **한복** *(han-bok)*. The operator's
wording is broad:

<Original
  ko="한복(개량한복 포함) 착용 시"
  en="When wearing hanbok (including modernised hanbok)."
  who="수원문화재단 — 화성행궁 관람 안내사항"
  whoEn="Suwon Cultural Foundation, Hwaseong Haenggung admission notes"
  date="2026-08-17"
  url="https://www.swcf.or.kr/?p=65" />

That is the same mechanism the Seoul palaces use, and the same arguments about what counts apply —
we have written up [what gets accepted and what gets refused](/en/guides/hanbok-free-palace-entry-what-counts/)
in detail.

Beyond hanbok, admission is free for visitors aged 65 and over with ID and for children under six.
Entry is also free on **Culture Day, the last Wednesday of each month** — but explicitly *not* during
the night opening, which is the one time most travellers would want it.

Here is where the sources stop agreeing. The foundation publishes the same exemption table on two of
its own sites. On the Suwon Hwaseong tourism site, the block listing the age, voting, disability and
multi-child exemptions carries the note **"내국인만 적용"** — Korean nationals only. On the
foundation's own site the equivalent note is narrower, marking only the multi-child clause as limited
to Suwon residents. Same operator, same table, two different scopes.

We are not going to guess which is current. The practical reading: **hanbok, 65-and-over with ID, and
under-six are the three a foreign visitor can reasonably expect to be honoured**, and even those get
decided at the ticket window. Bring the ID. Budget the ₩2,000 anyway.

## Night, and what it costs

From **1 May to 1 November 2026** the palace opens again in the evening, Friday to Sunday and on
public holidays, 18:00–21:30 with last entry at 21:00. The foundation notes the dates can move at its
discretion. Today falls inside that window, but it closes for the winter and the daytime hours are
all you get from November.

The wall, of course, does not close at all. If the night opening is finished for the year or you have
missed the last entry, the stream and the water gate are lit and free, and that is the stretch the
drama used anyway.

## Getting there, and the name that sends people to the wrong city

Suwon Station sits on **Seoul Subway Line 1** and takes about an hour from Seoul Station with no
change. Using the city's published fare rule — a ₩1,550 base fare with a card, plus ₩100 for every
5 km past the first 10 km — the ride works out at roughly ₩2,200–2,400. That is a figure we
calculated from the rule rather than one quoted on a fare table, so treat it as an estimate; the
[full fare structure is here](/en/now/seoul-transport-fares-subway-bus-taxi/).

The fortress is not at the station. It is about 2 km north, and the walk is along an ordinary
arterial road. Take a bus or a taxi for that leg.

One more trap, and it is the worst one. **화성시** *(Hwa-seong-si)* is Hwaseong City — a separate
municipality roughly 30 km away, with its own government and its own cultural foundation. English
maps and booking sites render it "Hwaseong," identically to the fortress. If you are searching in
English, search **"Suwon Hwaseong"** with Suwon attached, every time. Accommodation booked in
Hwaseong City is not near the wall and there is no easy transfer.

If you would rather ask a person: the Suwon General Tourist Information Centre is near Exit 4 of
Suwon Station, open 09:00–18:00, with English, Chinese and Japanese interpretation available.

## What this article does not claim

Fees, hours and the night-opening dates are as published by the Suwon Cultural Foundation on
2026-08-17 and were read from the operator's own pages, not from aggregators. Korean admission fees
change by municipal ordinance, and this one was last raised in February 2025 — check before you
budget precisely.

The filming locations are reported by Kyunghyang Shinmun from the neighbourhood, not confirmed by the
production. We have not walked these lanes ourselves. We are not naming the café or the blue-gate
house by address, and we would ask you not to either — one of them is a home.

The Seoul–Suwon fare is calculated from the published distance formula, not quoted. Exact ticketed
fares differ by card type and entry station.

Photographs on this page are Korea Tourism Organization aerial images of Suwon Hwaseong. They show
the fortress, not the drama's shooting locations, and we have captioned them for what they are. We
requested images of Hwaseong Haenggung as well; what arrived was a hotel of a similar name, and those
four frames were discarded rather than published.
