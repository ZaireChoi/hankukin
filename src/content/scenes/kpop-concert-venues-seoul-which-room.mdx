237 and 16,601.

Those are the seat counts of two rooms in the Seoul area where K-pop concerts happen, printed by the organisations that run them. One is a fan-shaped hall whose fixed seats carry no numbers at all. The other is a domed baseball stadium. Both turn up in English guides under the same four words — *K-pop concert venue* — and the name on your ticket will not tell you which kind of evening you have bought.

That matters less for the show than for the two hours around it: which station, which gate of which park, and whether anything is still running when 15,000 people come out of the doors at once. [Getting the ticket is a separate set of walls](/en/scenes/kpop-concert-tickets-korea-foreigners/), and we have written that one. This is what the ticket does not say.

## Five of the rooms are in the same park

Olympic Park in Songpa-gu is one address, 424 Olympic-ro, and the state company that runs it lists five hireable performance rooms inside it. They are not tiers of one building. They are five separate buildings, and the largest holds sixty-three times what the smallest does.

| Room | Also called | Seats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| KSPO DOME | 올림픽체조경기장 (Olympic Gymnastics Arena) | 15,000 |
| Ticketlink Live Arena | 핸드볼경기장, formerly 올림픽펜싱경기장 | 5,003 |
| Olympic Hall | — | 2,452 |
| Woori Financial Art Hall | 올림픽역도경기장 (Olympic Weightlifting Gymnasium) | 1,184 |
| Muse Live | — | 237 |

The operator's own pages tell you what each room is for, and they do it by naming who has played there. KSPO DOME's page lists BTS, EXO, Seventeen and IU. The Ticketlink Live Arena page lists Lee Seung-hwan, Park Jung-hyun, Red Velvet and BTOB. Muse Live describes itself as a room for indie bands and new artists, with fixed fan-shaped seating and — the detail worth holding on to — no seat numbers at all.

So "my concert is at Olympic Park" is not an answer. Three subway lines reach the park, at three stations and four exits between them: lines 5 and 9 both stop at Olympic Park station, exits 3 and 4; line 8 stops at Mongchontoseong station, exit 1; line 9 also stops at Hanseong Baekje station, exit 2. The park's own bus table does not sort routes by the park either — it sorts them by which numbered gate they serve, East Gate 1, South Gate 2, South Gate 4. Find the building before you pick the exit.

## Three of them changed names, and the old names still work

Three of the five rooms carry a name the building was not born with, and in two cases the new name belongs to a company. The operator prints both, which is the only reason we can tell you this: its page headings read 우리금융아트홀(올림픽역도경기장) and 티켓링크 라이브 아레나(핸드볼경기장), new name first, old name in brackets. The fencing hall became the handball hall before it became the arena named after a ticketing company.

The clearest evidence that the old identity is still live is the rate card. Woori Financial Art Hall is a 1,184-seat musical theatre now, remodelled over two years. Its published hire rates still include a line for 역도경기 *(yeok-do-gyeong-gi)* — a weightlifting competition, ₩221,000 a day. The building has not forgotten what it was built for, even if the sign out front says a bank.

This is the same problem we keep running into in a different costume: [English pages in Korea are often an older copy rather than a translation](/en/now/english-pages-korea-out-of-date/). If a search result gives you "Olympic Gymnastics Arena" and your ticket says "KSPO DOME", those are one building. If a taxi driver does not recognise the sponsor name, the old name is the one that works.

## Two more rooms, on opposite sides of the city

**Gocheok Sky Dome** is a domed baseball stadium in Guro-gu, in the far west, at 430 Gyeongin-ro — and if you can read [the number in a Korean road address](/en/hangul/korean-address-road-name-building-number/), that one tells you how far along the road it sits. It seats 16,601, which makes it the largest indoor room in this article, and it has 484 parking spaces. Those two numbers sit next to each other on the same page of the operator's site, and together they are the whole argument for the subway.

The useful fact about it is the walk. The operator's directions page gives four stations and only one of them is close: 구일역 *(gu-il-yeok)*, line 1, about 150 metres, roughly three minutes. Guro is a twenty-minute walk and Gaebong about fifteen. The page then does something we rarely see a venue do — it tells you to get off further away on purpose. Coming from Seoul or from Suwon and Anyang, it says, alight at Guro to avoid the crush; coming from Incheon or Bucheon, alight at Gaebong. A venue that publishes crowd-avoidance advice is telling you something about the exit.

**Jamsil Indoor Stadium**, in the south-east at 25 Olympic-ro, hosted the basketball and volleyball at the 1988 Olympics and is now the Seoul Samsung Thunders' home court. Two lines reach it, and which one is better depends on which building you want: from line 2, exits 6 and 7, the indoor stadium is about 700 metres; from line 9, exit 9, about 600. Line 2 is the shorter walk to the main stadium and line 9 the shorter walk to this one. The complex is large enough that 600 metres of walking is the normal case, not a mishap.

One thing we noticed while reading the indoor stadium's own page, and we are going to say it plainly rather than tidy it away. The city gives that room's capacity three times on one page and gets three different answers: 11,044 seats in the specification table, 11,032 in the floor-plan block below it, and 13,000 in the sentence at the top. The floor area is given twice, as 20,096 m² and 26,096 m². We have used 11,044 in the chart because it sits in the table headed 시설규모, and we are telling you the rest because a number you cannot reconcile is a number you should not lean on. Judge how far back your seat is from the seating chart attached to your ticket, not from a capacity figure.

## And one that is not in Seoul

Inspire Arena is the room that has taken the biggest touring shows of the last couple of years, and it is not in Seoul. It stands at 127 Gonghangmunhwa-ro on Yeongjong Island in Incheon — the same island as the airport — inside a resort. Its own page describes it as Korea's first multi-purpose indoor performance venue and states a capacity of **up to** 15,000, adding that the seating size can be negotiated with whoever hires it. That is not evasiveness; it is a variable room. But it does mean "15,000-seat arena" is a ceiling rather than a count.

Here is the part that catches people. The resort's own directions page offers three ways in: drive and park, valet, or the free shuttle buses. It does not list a train. If you are coming straight from a flight this is fine — better than fine, because the shuttle serves both airport terminals — and our [airport-into-Seoul comparison](/en/now/incheon-airport-to-seoul-train-bus-taxi/) is about a journey you may not need to make at all on arrival day. If you are coming from a hotel in central Seoul on a concert evening, read the next section before you book that hotel.

## Leaving is the part to plan

The shuttle timetables are published, and reading them backwards is the exercise that matters.

The Hongdae–Myeongdong route, added on 1 June 2026, runs three loops a day. The last one leaves the resort at 5:30pm, passes Hongdae at 6:40pm and Myeongdong at 7:10pm, and is back at the resort by 8:30pm. So the last shuttle *into* the arena from central Seoul leaves Myeongdong at 7:10pm — and after the show there is no Hongdae or Myeongdong shuttle at all.

The later option is a different route. A loop leaves the resort at 10:00pm and reaches Namguro station at 11:00pm and Daerim station at 11:07pm, both of which put you back on the Seoul subway network. The airport shuttles run until 10:20pm for Terminal 1 and 10:50pm for Terminal 2.

The same page adds its own warning for concert-goers: because of the seat limit you may not get the departure you wanted, so allow extra time. Forty seats a bus, an arena that seats up to 15,000, and — on the three routes into Seoul — between three and five departures in an entire day. The arithmetic is not subtle.

None of that is a reason to avoid the venue. It is a reason to decide, before you buy the ticket, whether you are staying on the island, driving, or catching the 10:00pm loop to Daerim. If the answer turns out to be a taxi across the bridge at midnight, that is a fare on a [late-night meter with a surcharge and possibly an intercity surcharge on top](/en/now/seoul-transport-fares-subway-bus-taxi/) — added by the meter, not negotiated.

For the Seoul venues the equivalent question is the last train, and the operators do not answer it on the venue page. Two night bus routes do appear in official access tables, though: N31 passes Olympic Park on a route running via Mongchontoseong and Jamsil, and N13 is listed among the blue trunk buses serving the Jamsil sports complex.

## The biggest stage in the country is a building site

If an older English page tells you to see a concert at Jamsil Olympic Main Stadium — 65,599 seats, 100,000 capacity, Kim Swoo-geun's 1984 building — that page is out of date. The city's booking page opens with a notice:

No reopening date is published — only a month when the block on bookings is expected to lift, and an explicit note that it may move.

It is worth reading the closed building's own description before it comes back, because it says something none of the newer rooms say. The entrances, it explains, were distributed across 54 points so that a crowd of 100,000 could get out within 30 minutes. Whoever drew that stadium — construction started in 1976 — treated leaving as a design problem with a number attached to it.

Two caveats, since we are asking you to trust a number. That 54 is from the prose; the floor-plan block on the same page counts 68 external entrances, and we cannot tell you which one the 30-minute claim was calculated against. And nobody publishes any figure like it for the rooms that are open today. That absence is why the two sections above this one had to be written from bus timetables instead.

## The timetables we did not open

**We did not check a single last train.** Every "how do you get home" line above is a published shuttle timetable or a night-bus route number lifted from an official access table. We have not opened a station timetable for Guil, Jamsil or Olympic Park, and we do not know what hours N31 and N13 currently run. Check the last departure before you need it, not after.

**Capacity is not tickets on sale**, and we have not matched any venue to a specific show. A stage at one end sells fewer seats than the figures here, and Inspire says its seating is negotiable. Which building your concert is in is the ticket page's job.

**Three official pages contradicted themselves, and we have said which figure we took**: Jamsil Indoor Stadium's three capacities, the main stadium's 54 entrances against 68, and Inspire's address — its own site gives both 영종구 (23358) and 중구 (22382), and we used the footer's 영종구. Gocheok's directions page would not reopen for us on a second attempt, so its opening date and its "first domed stadium" line are not here.

**There is no photograph.** Our only Seoul concert-venue images are Olympic Park's picnic ground — benches, bare trees, no arena in any frame. A bench cannot tell you which of five buildings to walk to.
