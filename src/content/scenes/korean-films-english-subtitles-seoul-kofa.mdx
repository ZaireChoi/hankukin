Two pages on the Korean Film Council's English site are called **Screenings with Eng. Subtitles**. We opened both on 19 August 2026.

One was showing the week of **1–7 May 2025**. The other was showing **15–21 December 2019**.

Neither is broken. They load, they list films, they look maintained. They are simply not about this week, and nothing on either page says so. That is the official English answer to a question a lot of people arrive in Korea holding: *I would like to watch a Korean film in a Korean cinema — where can I understand it?*

## The question is sharper than it sounds

A Hollywood film in Seoul is easy. It plays in its own language with Korean text along the bottom, and if you speak English you are already fine.

A Korean film is the opposite problem, and it is the one nobody warns you about, because the people writing the warnings are not the people who need them. A cinema subtitles a film so its audience can follow a language it does not speak. The audience for a Korean film in Korea speaks Korean, so there is nothing to add — and the one cinema in Seoul that sets its policy out in writing says precisely that, in the section below.

Which means the ticket is not the hard part. Finding the screening is.

## One place says so out loud, and marks it

Cinematheque KOFA is the cinema inside the Korean Film Archive in Sangam, western Seoul. It runs two screens in the basement, and it publishes a legend explaining exactly what you are going to get.

That second clause is the whole asymmetry, in one line. Everything foreign gets Korean text. Korean films get nothing — unless someone has made a subtitle file, and when they have, the listing says **E**.

The English side of the site defines the same mark in its programme legend: *E — English Subtitles*. And on individual film pages it goes further and writes it into the title, so there is no mark to squint at. The archive's page for the 2024 film *Citizen of a Kind* is headed **Citizen of a Kind [English Subtitles]**, with an E beside each of its showtimes.

Two habits follow from the grid. Look for the E, and then open the film's own page to confirm it, because the film page spells the words out and the calendar only shows a letter.

## What the archive is, and what it is not

Being honest about this is the difference between a good afternoon and a wasted trip across the city.

**It is not where you see this month's release.** The programme is classics, retrospectives, arthouse and archive prints — the sort of thing you would otherwise never get on a screen. A large share of it is not Korean at all: the archive runs international seasons and co-organised festivals alongside its Korean ones, which is precisely why its subtitle legend needed a clause about foreign films in the first place. Korean titles come round in curated blocks rather than continuously.

**It is free.** The English page states it flatly: *Enjoy all screening for free.* Not discounted, not free for foreigners, free.

**It is closed more than most cinemas.** Open Tuesday to Saturday, closed every Sunday and Monday, plus New Year's Day, the archive's own foundation day on 18 January, Labour Day on 1 May, and the Seollal and Chuseok holidays. If your one free afternoon in Seoul is a Sunday, this article is not for that afternoon.

**It starts on time and means it.** The page says screenings begin exactly on time and that entry may be refused once a film has started. It also asks for no food, no drink except bottled water, and no phone screens at all. Anyone who has been to a cinematheque in Europe will recognise the temperature of that paragraph; anyone expecting multiplex latitude should recalibrate.

The safest way in is the box office on the day. It opens one hour before the first film, takes up to two tickets per person, and closes five minutes before each screening. There is an online route as well — the English page describes booking up to five days ahead, opening daily at 11:00 — but see the last section before you rely on it.

## Getting there, and one wrong digit

The building is at **400 World Cup buk-ro, Mapo-gu, Seoul 03925**. In Korean, to show a driver: **서울 마포구 월드컵북로 400**. (Spelling it *buk-ro* rather than *bungno* is the archive following the official romanisation rule, which we have [taken apart elsewhere](/en/hangul/korean-romanization-jongno-not-jongro/) — it is the address as printed, so use it as printed.)

It sits in the DMC media cluster beside MBC, and the nearest station is Digital Media City. The fare is an ordinary [Seoul subway fare](/en/now/seoul-transport-fares-subway-bus-taxi/), but the walk at the far end is roughly fifteen minutes through an office district, so it is an errand rather than a stop-off. Budget the time both ways.

Now the digit. The archive's own English facilities page prints the Sangam telephone number as **+81 (0)2-3153-2001**. The footer of the same website prints it as **+82 (0)2 3153-2001**.

+82 is Korea. +81 is Japan. The number itself is identical, so the footer is the one to trust and the facilities page has a typo — but if you dial the page you are reading, from abroad, you reach the wrong country. We mention it not to score a point off a good institution, but because it is a compact example of the thing this whole article is about: the English layer of a Korean organisation is maintained by fewer hands and checked by fewer eyes than the Korean layer, and it is the only layer most visitors ever see. We have [written before about how far behind those English pages run](/en/now/english-pages-korea-out-of-date/).

## The rest of the building, and the version you can watch from home

The same address holds the **Korean Film Museum**, which is also free — all exhibitions, permanent and special — and open Tuesday to Saturday, 10:30 to 19:00, with last entry 30 minutes before the end of a session. Its audio guide app runs in English as well as Korean, which is not true of every museum in Seoul. There is a film reference library on the same floors.

And if you are reading this from somewhere you cannot easily fly from: the archive links a **Korean Classic Film** channel on YouTube from its own front page, alongside a Korean animation channel and its official channel. Same institution, same prints, no opening hours and no closed Mondays. Subtitle availability varies title by title, so check the individual video rather than the channel. For anyone who arrived at Korean cinema through [a K-drama filming location](/en/scenes/goblin-jumunjin-breakwater/) rather than through film festivals, that is a gentler doorway than a three-hour archive print on a Tuesday afternoon.

## Where our checking stopped

Everything above about hours, admission, the subtitle mark and the address comes from the Korean Film Archive's own pages, read on 19 August 2026. The two Korean Film Council pages were read the same day and the dates on them are quoted exactly as they appeared.

What we could not settle, and would rather say than paper over.

**We did not read the current schedule.** The archive's Korean schedule and calendar pages are assembled in the browser, and what our reader received was a page years out of date — the subtitle legend was intact, but the listings beside it were not this year's. We have therefore quoted no screening, no date and no film time from it. Check the schedule yourself before you travel across the city.

**We could not open a notice that may matter.** The archive has published a notice titled 「시네마테크KOFA 온라인 예매 절차 변경 안내」 — *notice of a change to Cinematheque KOFA's online booking procedure.* The page would not render for us, so we do not know what changed or when. The English about page still describes the five-day, 11:00 system, and it may well be current. We are telling you the box office exists because that is the part no procedural change removes.

**The fifteen-minute walk is approximate.** The archive's Korean directions page would not render for us either. Fifteen minutes is the figure that page is indexed as carrying, and it matches the distance on a map, but we have not read it on the page itself and we have not walked it. Treat it as a rough allowance, not a timetable.

**We have not verified what any multiplex does.** CGV, Lotte Cinema and Megabox all publish their schedules through pages that build in the browser, and we could not read them. We are not claiming those chains never screen a Korean film with English subtitles. We are reporting something narrower and, we think, more useful: the public body that publishes a page for exactly that purpose was, on the day we looked, fifteen months behind on it.

**On admission and hours.** Free admission and the Tuesday-to-Saturday pattern are what the archive's English pages state, and public institutions revise both. Nothing here is a promise made by us; it is a record of what those pages said on one dated day.
