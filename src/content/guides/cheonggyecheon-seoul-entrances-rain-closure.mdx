Cheonggyecheon is not a park with a stream in it. It is a stream channel with a walkway in it, and the difference matters when the weather turns.

The walkway sits several metres below the road. When it rains, Seoul's city-centre stormwater drains into that channel through sluice gates set into the walls — the gates are underneath the bridges. The operator's public guidance to visitors is blunt about what this means:

> Cheonggyecheon has the characteristic that when it rains the water level rises sharply and the walkway is suddenly submerged. … In particular, the area under the bridges is extremely dangerous because the sluice gates open suddenly when it rains, so please do not shelter under a bridge from the rain.

That is the one sentence worth carrying with you. The dry, covered spot you would instinctively run to in a downpour is the spot the water arrives through.

## The numbers, from the operator

Seoul Facilities Corporation (서울시설공단) runs the stream and publishes the figures.

| | |
|---|---|
| Total length | 8.12 km |
| Restored section | 5.84 km |
| Walkway, both banks combined | 20 km |
| Bridges rebuilt | 22 |
| Lights | 10,419 |
| Restoration work | 1 July 2003 – 30 September 2005 |
| Admission | Free, no ticket, no gate |

Visitor counts for January to May 2026 average **62,000 people a day**. On the first Sunday it opened in October 2005 the daily average for that month was 129,000. It is not a hidden corner of the city.

## Getting down to the water: 65 ways in, 42 of them stairs

This is the fact that changes people's day and almost nobody publishes it.

| Type of entrance | Number |
|---|---|
| Staircases | 42 |
| Ramps | 21 |
| Lifts | 2 |
| Vehicle access control points | 2 |
| **Total** | **65** |

Two lifts, over 5.84 km. If you are pushing a pram, using a wheelchair, dragging a suitcase, or simply do not want to climb back out of a channel at the end of a long day, the entrance you happen to be standing above is a coin flip.

The operator publishes a mobility-access route broken into nine bridge-to-bridge segments — 모전교~삼일교, 삼일교~세운교, and so on — under **이동약자 노선안내** (*idong-yakja noseon annae*, "route guidance for people with reduced mobility") on its Cheonggyecheon site. Those pages load their maps interactively rather than as plain text, so check them on a phone before you set out rather than relying on a description.

The practical version: **ramps cluster at the big road bridges**. Stairs are the default everywhere else.

## When rain closes it — the actual thresholds

The control room monitors the streambed by CCTV around the clock, all year, with safety staff on the ground. Seoul's formal flood-defence season runs **15 May to 15 October**, but the 24-hour watch does not stop outside it.

Closure escalates on published triggers:

| Stage | Trigger |
|---|---|
| Normal (관심) | Standing watch for sudden rainfall |
| Internal stage 1 | Forecast of more than 5 mm maximum rainfall in 6 hours |
| Internal stage 2 | Forecast of 30 mm or more in 6 hours |
| Seoul stage 1 (주의) | Heavy rain **advisory** — 60 mm in 3 hours, or 110 mm in 12 hours |
| Seoul stage 2 (경계) | Heavy rain **warning** — 90 mm in 3 hours, or 180 mm in 12 hours |
| Seoul stage 3 (심각) | Flood warning — Han River level reaches 10.5 m |

Read the top of that table again. **Internal stage 1 is a forecast of 5 mm.** That is not a storm; that is an ordinary grey afternoon in July. Closures are frequent and they are not rare-event closures.

When it happens, you will hear a public-address announcement in Korean and see staff moving people toward the exits. The instruction is to leave by the **nearest** exit, not the one you came in by. Twenty-seven life-ring stations are installed along the stream, mostly at the tops and bottoms of the stairs.

Closures and re-openings are posted on the operator's Cheonggyecheon notice board under the heading **안전특보** (*anjeon teukbo*, "safety alert"), with wording like **청계천 산책로 통제** (*sanchaengno tongje*, "walkway closed") or **통제 해제** (*tongje haeje*, "closure lifted"). If it has rained in the last few hours, that board is the thing to check — sometimes only part of the length reopens, which is posted as **부분 해제** (*bubun haeje*, "partially lifted").

## Things that are banned and catch people out

The operator lists prohibited activities. Some are obvious. Three are not:

- **No bicycles, no inline skates.** People arrive expecting the Han River cycle paths. Cheonggyecheon is a walkway. Wheels are not permitted on it.
- **No animals**, with guide dogs for disabled visitors explicitly excepted.
- **No smoking and no drinking alcohol** anywhere along it.

Also prohibited: fishing, swimming and bathing or anything similar, camping and cooking, sleeping rough and trading, and dumping waste.

## The water is engineered, and it is not drinkable

Cheonggyecheon does not run on its own. It is fed **40,000 tonnes a day of treated Han River water**, with capacity to push that to 120,000 tonnes, plus roughly **20,000 tonnes a day of groundwater** pumped out of subway tunnels and electrical facilities that would otherwise be discarded.

The operator states the result plainly: the water is clean enough for fish — Korean chub (버들치), pale chub (피라미) and carp (잉어) live in it — but **it fails drinking-water standards on coliform bacteria and turbidity, and must not be drunk.**

There are no taps in the channel. Bring water in with you, because getting out to buy some means finding a staircase.

## How far is it really

## The three official walking courses

If 5.84 km is more than you want, the operator names three segments:

| Course | Route | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 청계역사길 — history | Mojeongyo → Gwansugyo | 1.17 km |
| 청계활력길 — energy | Majeongyo (Gwangjang Market) → DDP | 0.68 km to Ogansugyo |
| 청계휴식길 — rest | Dumuldari → Yongdap Nadeulmokgyo | not published |

The routes are the operator's; the lengths in the third column are ours, added up from the published bridge spacings. Yongdap Nadeulmokgyo sits past the end of that spacing table, so the third course cannot be measured this way.

The middle one is the useful one for most visitors: it links [Gwangjang Market](/en/now/korean-street-food-hidden-pork-and-fish/) to Dongdaemun along the water instead of along the traffic.

Guided walks with a cultural interpreter are run separately by the Seoul Tourism Organization through its walking-tour service, not by the stream's operator.

## Do not walk the whole thing on a Monday

Both things worth reaching at the downstream end are closed on Mondays.

| | Hours | Closed | Admission |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cheonggyecheon Museum** 청계천박물관 | 09:00–18:00, all year | Mondays, 1 January | Free |
| **Panjatjip Theme Zone** 판잣집 테마존 | 10:00–18:00, all year | Mondays | Free |

The museum is at 청계천로 530, Seongdong-gu — it opened on 26 September 2005 alongside the restoration, and covers the stream's history and the restoration itself across a permanent gallery, with a café inside. The Theme Zone stands directly in front of it: it was built in 2008 as a re-creation of 1960s–70s shanty housing, and was rebuilt in 2022 keeping the exterior while changing the contents to ecology and sustainability displays.

Both sit at the far end, past Gosanjagyo. Walking 5.5 km to a locked door is an avoidable Monday.

## Fountains

Twelve in total: six wall fountains (청계폭포, the Samgak-dong water screen, 옥류천, 세운폭포, 오간수문, 리듬벽천), four jet fountains (at the source, Sewungyo, Ogansugyo and the Proposal Wall), one tunnel fountain at Biudanggyo, and the mist fountain at the Gwanggyo digital canvas.

Operating schedules for these are not published on the operator's overview page, and we could not verify them from a primary source. Assume they may be off.

## What this article does not claim

Every figure above — lengths, bridge spacings, entrance counts, rainfall stages, prohibited activities, water volumes, museum and theme-zone hours — comes from Seoul Facilities Corporation and the Seoul Metropolitan Government pages listed in the sources, read on 14 August 2026.

We have not walked the route to verify which specific entrances have ramps rather than stairs; the operator's segment maps are interactive and we could not extract them as text. We have not verified fountain operating times, seasonal light-installation dates, or the current status of any closure. Closure status changes within hours, so check the operator's notice board on the day.

We do not know what the water level will be when you arrive. Nobody does — that is the point of the control room.
