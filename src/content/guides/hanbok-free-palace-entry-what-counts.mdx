There is a version of a day in Seoul that goes like this.

You put on **hanbok** in the morning. You walk to the gate of a five-hundred-year-old palace, and you walk straight past the ticket window. Nobody charges you. You do it again at the next palace, and the next. At the last one — Deoksugung, which stays open until nine at night — you are still wearing it after dark, and the crowds have thinned, and the courtyard is quiet.

That day is free. All five palaces, every one of them, for the price of the clothes.

This is not a promotion or a seasonal event. It is written into the admission rules, listed alongside categories like state guests and registered guardians, in a line that reads simply: *those wearing hanbok*.

The question everyone asks next is whether it applies to foreign visitors. The Royal Palaces and Tombs Center answers that one directly on its own guideline page, and the reasoning is worth quoting because it explains the whole policy: the purpose of free admission for hanbok wearers is the popularisation and globalisation of hanbok, so it applies **regardless of nationality**.

So yes. The rule was designed with you in mind.

## The part rental shops don't always explain

Here is where people get caught. The palace does not assess whether your hanbok is expensive, traditional, or authentic. It assesses **shape** — and one requirement does most of the rejecting:

> You must wear both a top and a bottom.

A *jeogori* (저고리, the jacket) **and** a skirt or trousers. Both. This sounds obvious until you look at what is actually for rent and what people actually choose.

## What fails

| Outfit | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Jeans with a jeogori | **No** |
| Hanbok skirt with a T-shirt | **No** |
| A one-piece hanbok dress, no jeogori | **No** |
| A T-shirt-style modern hanbok top | **No** |
| A *durumagi* (overcoat) worn over regular clothes | **No** |

The one-piece case is the cruel one. Plenty of garments sold as hanbok are dresses — beautiful, comfortable, obviously hanbok-inspired. The guideline says a jeogori and a skirt are required, so the dress alone does not qualify.

The T-shirt-style top fails for the same underlying reason. The test for a jeogori is the **overlapping front collar** (여미는 깃) — the crossed panel that closes across the chest. If the top pulls over your head like a T-shirt, it has no overlapping collar, so it is not a jeogori, however hanbok-ish the fabric looks.

## What passes, including things people assume won't

This is the encouraging half, and it is more generous than the failures suggest.

**Modern hanbok counts.** The guideline states plainly that both traditional hanbok and *saenghwal hanbok* (생활한복, everyday or modernised hanbok) qualify. Age and authenticity are not the criteria. Shape is.

**No ribbon ties needed.** A jeogori without *goreum* — the long fabric ties — still counts. As long as the overlapping collar is there, how you fasten it is your business. Buttons, snaps, knots, all fine.

**Zips and loose ankles are fine on trousers.** Hanbok trousers traditionally have a wrapped waist and *daenim* ties at the ankle. Modern versions often have a zip and no ties. The guideline judges trousers by whether they approximate the *sapok* cut, so a zipped waist and bare ankles do not disqualify you.

**Skirts are unrestricted in closure.** Wrapped, unwrapped, either is accepted. A jeogori with a *heorichima* (허리치마, the high-waisted underskirt) qualifies.

**Summer ramie counts** if it otherwise meets the guideline.

There is one soft instruction attached to all of this: dress in a way that suits the dignity of a palace. Excessive exposure is discouraged. It is written as guidance rather than a measurable rule, which in practice means the staff at the gate exercise judgement.

## What it actually saves you

Modest amounts, honestly. This is not the reason to do it.

| | Adult admission |
|---|---|
| Gyeongbokgung | ₩3,000 |
| Changdeokgung (palace grounds) | ₩3,000 |
| Changgyeonggung | ₩1,000 |
| Deoksugung | ₩1,000 |
| Jongmyo Shrine | ₩1,000 |

All five in one day comes to ₩9,000 — and there is a combined ticket covering the four palaces plus Jongmyo for ₩6,000, valid six months. So the saving is real but small, and hanbok rental will cost you considerably more than the tickets.

Two notes worth having. **Changdeokgung's Huwon (Secret Garden) is a separate ₩5,000 ticket** and is excluded from the combined ticket; you also need the palace ticket on top. And the **last Wednesday of every month is Culture Day**, when the palaces are free for everyone, Huwon excepted.

## The mistake that ruins the day

Not the dress code. The closing days.

| Closed Mondays | Closed Tuesdays |
|---|---|
| Changdeokgung, Deoksugung, Changgyeonggung, Royal Tombs | Gyeongbokgung, Jongmyo |

People rent hanbok for the morning, photograph Gyeongbokgung, then walk to Changdeokgung and find it shut — or the reverse. If a closing day falls on a public holiday the site opens instead, and the closure shifts to the next non-holiday day.

Deoksugung and Changgyeonggung stay open until 21:00 year-round, which makes them the natural end of a hanbok day. Gyeongbokgung closes much earlier, and the last entry is an hour to an hour and a half before closing depending on the season.

## What this article does not claim

We are not recommending rental shops, and we have not verified any shop's claim that its garments qualify. The guideline is published by the Royal Palaces and Tombs Center; individual shops are not bound by it and may describe their stock loosely.

We also cannot promise how a specific outfit will be judged. The written rules are clear about tops, bottoms and collars, but the dignity clause is a matter of judgement made at the gate by staff, not by us.

If you want certainty, the safest combination is the plainest one: a jeogori with an overlapping collar, and a separate skirt or pair of trousers. That has never been the ambiguous case.
