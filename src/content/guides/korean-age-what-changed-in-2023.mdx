If you watch Korean drama with subtitles, you have probably hit this. A character says they are twenty-five. Later someone says they were born in 1999. The arithmetic does not work, and the subtitle does not explain.

The reason is that Korea spent a long time running more than one way of counting age at the same time, and only settled the question in 2023.

## The system you have been colliding with

In everyday Korean life, people used what is usually translated as **Korean age** (세는 나이): you are **one year old on the day you are born**, and you gain a year on **1 January**, not on your birthday.

Two consequences follow, and both feel strange from the outside.

**Everyone born in the same calendar year is the same age.** Birthdays do not affect it. This is not a quirk — it is the whole point, and it is why Korean social life is organised so tightly around birth year.

**A baby born on 31 December is two years old the next day.** One at birth, two at the new year.

Meanwhile, Korean law mostly used **international age** (만 나이) — zero at birth, plus one on each birthday — while a few statutes used a third method: current year minus birth year, ignoring the birthday entirely.

Three systems, in active simultaneous use. The government's own description of the situation is that it caused confusion and disputes.

## What the law did in 2023

On **28 June 2023**, amendments to the **Civil Act** and the **Framework Act on Administration** took effect. The rule they establish is short:

> Age in administrative and judicial contexts is calculated as international age — counting from the date of birth — and expressed in years. Unless a specific provision says otherwise, any age written in a law, a contract, or an official document is to be read as international age.

The official calculation, in the Ministry of Government Legislation's own wording:

- **Birthday has passed this year** → current year − birth year
- **Birthday has not passed yet** → current year − birth year − 1

That is it. It is the same arithmetic the rest of the world uses; the change was making it the default reading everywhere rather than one option among several.

## What did not change

This is the part most explanations skip.

**The law governs documents, not conversation.** It sets how age is interpreted in statutes, contracts and official paperwork. It does not — and could not — stop people saying the old number in daily speech, and many still do, particularly when working out seniority.

**Some statutes keep their own rule.** The amendment applies "unless there is a separate special provision." Certain laws continue to use year-of-birth counting for their own purposes.

So if you hear two different ages for the same person, neither is necessarily wrong. One may be the legal figure and the other the conversational one.

## Why the old system was so hard to drop

Korean has no neutral "you." Speech level, and often [the word you use for someone](/en/decode/oppa-hyung-unnie-noona/), depends on relative age — which is why a K-drama script is full of characters establishing who is older within the first few lines of meeting.

An age system where everyone born in the same year is exactly the same age makes that calculation trivial. Ask the birth year, and the social relationship resolves immediately. International age reintroduces ambiguity: two people born in the same year can be different ages for several months, which is precisely the situation the older system was built to avoid.

That is why the phrase you will still hear is **"몇 년생이세요?"** — *what year were you born?* — rather than a question about age at all.

## Working it out while you watch

If a character's birth year is mentioned and the number does not match the age they claimed:

| The character says | They probably mean |
|---|---|
| An age one higher than the maths | Korean age, and their birthday has passed |
| An age two higher than the maths | Korean age, and their birthday has not come yet |

Dramas set before mid-2023 use the old convention throughout, and many written after it still do in dialogue, because that is how people talk.

## What this article does not claim

We are describing the 2023 amendments as published by the Ministry of Government Legislation, checked on the date above. We are not giving legal advice, and we have not surveyed which specific statutes retained year-of-birth counting.

For the text of the provisions themselves, the ministry directs readers to the Korean Law Information Center, which publishes the Civil Act and the Framework Act on Administration in full.
