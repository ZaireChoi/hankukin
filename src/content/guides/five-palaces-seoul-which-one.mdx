Seoul has four royal palaces and one royal shrine, all within about forty minutes' walk of each other. Most first-time visitors intend to see all five and get through two and a half.

That is not a stamina problem. It is a scheduling one. **Two of them close on Mondays and two close on Tuesdays**, so whichever day you pick, something is shut. And only two stay open past six in the evening, which means the order you walk them decides whether your day ends at a locked gate or in an empty courtyard at dusk.

Here is what each one is, and how they fit together.

## The five, in one table

| | Closed | Adult ticket | Open until |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gyeongbokgung** | Tuesdays | ₩3,000 | 17:00–18:30 by season |
| **Changdeokgung** | Mondays | ₩3,000 (+₩5,000 Huwon) | 17:30–18:30 by season |
| **Changgyeonggung** | Mondays | ₩1,000 | **21:00** year-round |
| **Deoksugung** | Mondays | ₩1,000 | **21:00** year-round |
| **Jongmyo Shrine** | Tuesdays | ₩1,000 | varies |

The combined ticket is **₩6,000** and covers all five for six months. Buying them separately comes to ₩9,000, so the pass pays for itself if you see three or more. It does **not** include Changdeokgung's Secret Garden, which is a separate ₩5,000.

## Gyeongbokgung — the big one

<Figure src={gyeonghoeru} alt="Gyeonghoeru Pavilion at Gyeongbokgung reflected in its pond at dusk"
        caption="Gyeonghoeru, the banquet pavilion, sitting on an artificial island inside Gyeongbokgung."
        {...KHS} />

This is the one people picture. It was the primary palace of the Joseon dynasty, it is the largest, and it has the widest ceremonial spaces — Geunjeongjeon's stone courtyard, the Gyeonghoeru pavilion on its pond, the reconstructed crown prince's quarters to the east.

It is also the most crowded, and it closes earliest of the five. Both of those argue for going in the morning.

**Closed Tuesdays.** Last entry is an hour to an hour and a half before closing, depending on season — in winter that means a 16:00 cutoff, which arrives sooner than people expect.

## Changdeokgung — the one to slow down in

Changdeokgung was the palace the kings actually preferred to live in, and it shows. Where Gyeongbokgung is laid out on a grid, Changdeokgung follows the hills. Buildings sit where the ground allowed rather than where symmetry demanded.

The Huwon — the Secret Garden behind it — is the reason most people come, and it is the one thing here that needs planning: separate ticket, separate booking, and it sells out fast in spring and autumn. We wrote about that separately.

**Closed Mondays.** Note that this is the opposite of Gyeongbokgung, which is the single most common planning mistake we see.

## Changgyeonggung — the quiet one with the strange history

Smaller, greener, and far less visited than its neighbours. It adjoins Changdeokgung directly, so the two make a natural pair on one walk.

Its modern history is grim in a way worth knowing: during the colonial period it was converted into a zoo and botanical garden, and the name was downgraded from *gung* (palace) to *won* (garden). The restoration is relatively recent.

At **₩1,000** it is the cheapest way into a palace in Seoul, and it stays open until **21:00**.

## Deoksugung — the one that ends the day

<Figure src={geoncheonggung} alt="Geoncheonggung at Gyeongbokgung, a residence built in the late Joseon period"
        caption="Late-Joseon palace architecture. Deoksugung carries the same period's collision with the outside world, in a more visible form."
        {...KHS} />

Deoksugung is the smallest of the four palaces and the strangest, because it contains Western neoclassical buildings alongside traditional halls — the result of Korea's late-nineteenth-century opening. Seokjojeon, the stone hall, looks like it belongs in Europe.

It sits directly on City Hall Plaza, so it is the easiest to reach, and it is open until **21:00**. This makes it the natural last stop of a palace day: you arrive when the crowds have gone and the buildings are lit.

**Closed Mondays.**

## Jongmyo — not a palace

Jongmyo is the royal ancestral shrine, where the spirit tablets of the Joseon kings and queens are kept. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site and it does not look like the palaces at all — one immensely long, low, dark building, almost without ornament, at the end of a stone path.

If the palaces are about power on display, Jongmyo is about power kept quiet. It is the one people are most likely to skip and most likely to remember.

**Closed Tuesdays.**

## The order that works

<Figure src={sujeongjeon} alt="Sujeongjeon Hall at Gyeongbokgung"
        caption="Sujeongjeon at Gyeongbokgung. Morning light, before the courtyards fill."
        {...KHS} />

**If you go on a Monday** — Gyeongbokgung and Jongmyo are open; the other three are shut. Do those two properly rather than rushing.

**If you go on a Tuesday** — the reverse. Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung and Deoksugung. This is actually a good day: Changdeokgung and Changgyeonggung adjoin, and Deoksugung stays open late.

**Any other day**, this order holds up:

1. **Gyeongbokgung** in the morning — largest, most crowded, closes earliest
2. **Changdeokgung** at midday, with the Huwon slot you booked six days ago
3. **Changgyeonggung** straight after, since you can walk between them
4. **Deoksugung** in the evening — open to 21:00, and quiet by then

Jongmyo needs its own slot rather than being squeezed in; it deserves the attention and rushing it defeats the point.

## What this article does not claim

Hours and prices are as published by the Royal Palaces and Tombs Center on the date shown above. They change, and seasonal hour bands shift.

We have also not verified guide-tour requirements or reservation rules for Jongmyo, which historically has operated on a timed-entry system. Check the official site before going rather than relying on this page for that detail.
