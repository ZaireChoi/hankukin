The price first, because it is what you came for. From the operator's own page, read on 17 August 2026:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Adult (13 and over) | **₩29,000** |
| Child (3–12) | **₩23,000** |
| Senior (65 and over) | **₩23,000** — the same as a child |
| Under 36 months | Free, one child per accompanying adult |

There is also a 30% discount on a disability registration: for grades 1–3 it covers the holder and one companion, for grades 4–6 the holder only. Packages and group rates are excluded. Holders of a national merit disability rating at grades 1–3 get the same terms.

## The English page is right, and we are going to say so

We publish [an article about English pages in Korea being older copies of the Korean ones](/en/now/english-pages-korea-out-of-date/). It would be easy to keep finding evidence for that. So here is the opposite, checked the same afternoon: the tower's English admission page and its Korean one carry **the same two numbers**, and the English hours table matches the Korean one line for line, restaurant by restaurant.

If a Korean business has bothered to keep both current, that is worth a sentence.

## Two official websites, two different companies

This is the part that sends people to the wrong place.

Search for the tower and you will find **seoultower.co.kr**. It is an official site. It is not the observatory's. It now brands itself **YTN서울타워**, it covers the building, the plaza, the shops and the walking routes up Namsan — and its menu has **no ticket page at all**. "N서울타워" appears in its navigation under *매장안내*, the tenant directory.

The observatory has its own site, **nseoultower.co.kr**, with the prices above. Its error page tells you who runs it: complaints go to an address at **cj.net**.

So the tower is one silhouette on the skyline and two companies on paper — the same shape as [the lost-property system that is four centres and three operators](/en/now/lost-something-in-korea-what-to-do/). Neither arrangement is hidden. Neither is explained to you either.

Neither page is wrong. They describe different points on the same structure, and only one of them is a floor. **The mountain is doing most of the work**, which is also why the walk up is the real climb and the tower is the last 130 metres of it.

## Hours, and the half-hour that catches people

Open every day of the year. From the Korean timetable, matched by the English one:

| | Weekdays | Weekends & holidays |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Observatory** | 10:00–22:30 | 10:00–23:00 |
| N Gift Shop, Plaza 5F | 10:00–22:00 | 10:00–22:00 |
| n.GRILL | 12:00–22:00 | 12:00–22:00 |
| THE PLACE DINING | 11:00–22:00 | 11:00–22:00 |
| N Terrace | 11:00–20:00 | 11:00–20:00 |

**Last admission is 30 minutes before closing.** That is stated on both language versions and it is the line that ruins evenings: arriving at 22:20 on a weekday is arriving after the observatory has stopped letting people in, even though the building is still lit and busy.

Two more things from the same pages. Hours change with weather and operating schedule, so a windy night is a real risk rather than a disclaimer. And the 5th-floor viewing area rotates — one full turn takes **1 hour 40 minutes**, which is slow enough that you will not notice it and fast enough that your photo backdrop is not the one you sat down with.

## Buying at the gate, or before you go

The official site does not sell you a ticket. It links out to partners — Trip.com and the Korea Tourism Organization's site are both on its English front page. So "buy direct from the operator online" is not one of your options here, which is worth knowing before you go looking for it.

On Klook, opened today, the plain observatory ticket showed **US$14.19** against a stated retail of **US$20.50**, with a cable-car package at **US$26.75** and a fast-track ticket at **US$38.89**. We are printing the currencies as each seller printed them and not converting, because the rate moves and because the amount you are charged depends on the currency you select at checkout.

**The terms are the thing to read, not the discount.** On that listing: no cancellation, no refund, no date change, for any reason. It is an open ticket valid until 31 December 2026, and if you cannot redeem it because the site is at capacity you are told to come back another day rather than get your money back.

That trade is reasonable for a fixed plan and bad for a maybe. If your Seoul evening is still hypothetical, the gate is the flexible option and you already know what it costs.

## Getting up there

Three ways, and the tower's own site organises its directions page around exactly this choice: on foot, by cable car, by public transport, by car.

The bus most visitors want is the **Namsan circular bus 01**, boarded at Chungmuro station (lines 3 and 4) or Dongguk University station (line 3), every 6 to 9 minutes, running 06:30–23:00. For the cable car, leave Myeongdong station by exit 3 and follow the signs for Namsan Tunnel 3 to the Namsan Oreum lift, which carries you up to the cable car plaza.

If you want dinner at n.GRILL you have to telephone — **02-3455-9297 / 9298**. There is no online booking path for it and no package that includes it.

## What this article does not claim

**We did not go up.** Everything above is read from the operator's published pages and one seller's listing, on 17 August 2026. We have not verified the queue, the view, or whether the fast-track ticket saves you the time it charges for.

**We did not check the cable car's own operator or price.** It is a separate business from the tower and we have not read its pages.

**We have not compared it with Seoul Sky or 63.** Their pages did not load for us today, and a comparison built on one of the three would be worse than no comparison.

**The 400-metre figure is the operator's, not a survey.** It appears as prose on the Observation Deck page rather than in the Tower data table, and the two pages do not reconcile the numbers themselves. We have quoted both rather than pick one.
