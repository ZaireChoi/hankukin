Watch any Korean drama for ten minutes and you will notice that characters keep addressing each other with words the subtitles either leave in Korean or translate as "sir." Almost nobody says a plain first name to someone older than them.

This is not politeness for its own sake. Korean is structured so that there is often no neutral option available — and the words that fill the gap are the words for siblings.

## The four words, and why there are four

They encode two things at once: whether the person you are addressing is older than you, and the genders of *both* of you.

| You are | They are older, male | They are older, female |
|---|---|---|
| Male | 형 *hyung* | 누나 *nuna* |
| Female | 오빠 *oppa* | 언니 *unnie* |

There is no equivalent set for people younger than you. That is the asymmetry that drives everything else: you need a word for someone above you, and you generally do not need one for someone below you — their name works.

The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture lists exactly this group — 할아버지, 할머니, 아저씨, 아주머니, 형, 언니, 오빠, 누나 — as the **친족어 대용형**, the "kinship-word substitute" category of address. It is a recognised type of address term, not slang.

## Why a name alone is often unusable

Korean address terms come in several types, and each one is doing a different job:

| Type | Example | What it signals |
|---|---|---|
| Name | 채린아, 송채린 씨, 송채린 님 | Peer or below; 씨/님 raise it |
| Job title | 김 과장, 김 과장님 | Rank in an organisation |
| Kinship | 할아버지, 형수, 형수씨 | Family relation |
| Pronoun | 자네, 자기 | Limited, relationship-specific |
| Generic kinship | 아줌마, 아저씨 | Stranger, treated as family-ish |

The encyclopedia notes that the same person gets a different word depending on setting and closeness: **송채린** is *채린아* at home but *송채린 씨* or *송채린 님* at school or work. Between people the same age, it is *송윤근 씨* until they become close, then *윤근아*.

One trap for learners: attaching 씨 to a **surname only** — 김 씨, 이 씨 — carries no respect at all, despite looking like the same construction.

So if the person is older, using a bare name is out. If they have no job title, the title route is out. What is left is a kinship word.

## Which is why the barista is your sister

The 2018 National Institute of Korean Language analysis puts the mechanism bluntly: as families got smaller and people spent more time with non-relatives than relatives, **kinship vocabulary moved in to fill the shortage of general address terms**. Korean society is unusually sensitive to job titles, and there is simply no comfortable word for a stranger who does not have one. So 형, 언니, 아주머니, 아저씨, 할머니, 할아버지 do the work — or people give up on a word entirely and say **저기요**, roughly "over there," which is not an address term at all.

The paper singles out one recent development: 삼촌 (uncle), 이모 (maternal aunt) and 언니 have become standard for addressing service staff. And the selection rule is not what you would expect:

> 이모, 언니 are used regardless of whether [the person] is older or younger than the speaker. How old the speaker is does not matter; if the other person is treated as roughly middle-aged, 이모, and if treated as relatively young, 언니.

You are not stating a fact about age. You are assigning a role.

The same source flags 아버지 / 어머니 — literally *father* and *mother* — spreading as address terms for elderly strangers, particularly on television. The author, a professor of Korean linguistics, records finding this personally unacceptable, and then records with some alarm that the standard dictionary has already added the sense. That argument is live, not settled.

## The historical part that will surprise you

These four words have not always divided up the way they do now.

The *Dictionnaire Coréen–Français* of 1880 — the first substantial Korean dictionary, compiled by French missionaries — defines **형** as the word for an older sibling **of the same sex, for men and women alike**. A woman's older sister was 형. So was her brother's wife.

**언니 is not in it at all.** According to the 2018 analysis, 언니 first shows up in a dictionary in 1915, and the 1920 *Joseoneo Sajeon* defines it as "a small child's word for 형" — a nursery version, used by boys and girls both. Only later did the pair split into 형 for men and 언니 for women.

The evidence the paper offers for this is a detail worth keeping: the standard Korean primary-school graduation song, written by Yun Seok-jung, contains the line *빛나는 졸업장을 타신 언니께* — "to the 언니 who received the shining diploma" — sung by children to graduating pupils of either sex.

The one place standard Korean still has women saying 형 is the frozen form **형님**, used for a husband's older sister or an older sister-in-law.

## The one that catches everyone: 압존법

If you are in front of your grandparents and need to mention your father, you do not honorify him. You say 아비 — the plain, lowered form.

This is **압존법**, and the logic is comparative: your father outranks you, but not your grandfather, and in your grandfather's presence the second fact wins. Dramas set in traditional or wealthy families use this constantly, and subtitles almost never mark it.

## What to actually do

If you are learning, the rule that survives contact with reality is this: **ask the birth year early, then use the word, not the name.** That is why K-drama characters spend their first scene together establishing who is older — the conversation cannot proceed smoothly until it is settled.

And note the direction of travel. The 2018 paper observes that modern Korean honorifics are shifting from expressing **vertical authority** toward expressing **horizontal closeness**. Adult children calling their parents 엄마 and 아빠 was contested when the 2011 standards were drafted; it is now unremarkable in private.

## What this article does not claim

Everything above about address-term categories, the 1880 and 1920 dictionary entries, the service-industry usage and 압존법 comes from the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture and a 2018 analysis published by the National Institute of Korean Language, checked on the date above.

We are **not** claiming anything about the romantic use of 오빠 — for a boyfriend, or by fans toward male idols. That usage is real and widely discussed, but neither source we opened documents it, so we are not describing it here as fact.

We have also not verified how any of this is handled in North Korean standard language, in regional dialects (the sources note dialect forms exist and differ), or in specific companies that have deliberately replaced titles with 님 for everyone.

The 2018 analysis is a signed academic column published in the institute's journal 『새국어생활』, and it is explicit that it is *arguing for changes* to the 2011 *Standard Language Etiquette* guidelines. Where we quote its criticisms, those are the author's positions, not settled government policy. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture carries the same caveat on every article: entries reflect the academic view of the named author and may differ from the official position of the Academy of Korean Studies.
